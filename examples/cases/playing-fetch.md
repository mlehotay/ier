# Playing Fetch

## Play as Broad Continuation and Compressible Failure

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.10.7)**
*Illustrative Example · Non-Normative · Non-Criterial*

## Status, Scope, and Authority

This is a worked interpretation of a familiar activity. It introduces no new
IER criteria, behavioral mechanisms, or diagnostic claims about animal
experience. Canonical documents govern the theory.

The continuation of an activity involving a dog and a human does not establish one experiential
regime or a shared Unified Experiential Field (UEF). The account below describes
observable play and possible human interpretations; it does not identify the
dog's experience or its primary aim.

## The Game Can Survive a Miss

A person throws a ball. A dog chases it, retrieves it, and returns for another
throw. Actual fetch is often less tidy: the dog may overshoot, lose sight of
the ball, become distracted, or return empty-handed. The person may throw badly
or pause to find the toy.

These deviations can remain part of the game. Missing a catch need not end
play, whereas a missed step in a more demanding activity may require an
immediate repair.

## Situation and Affordances

A *situation*, in ordinary terms here, is the activity as organized for a
participant: who and what are involved, what seems to be happening, and what
can be done next. An *affordance* is an available opportunity for action, such
as throwing, chasing, searching, pausing, or inviting another throw.

A forgiving episode of fetch leaves many such opportunities open. The dog
can approach indirectly; the person can retrieve the ball instead; both can
pause and resume. These are possible ways of preserving the activity, rather
than a requirement that every episode of fetch behave alike.

## Compressible Failure

Suppose the dog overshoots the ball. The immediate retrieval has failed, but
little may need to change for play to continue. The person can wait, gesture,
or throw again. In this illustrative use, the failure is *compressible*: it can
be absorbed without reorganizing the whole interaction.

That tolerance has limits. Fatigue, injury, loss of interest, or an unavailable
toy may interrupt or end the game. Whether a deviation is absorbable depends
on the actual participants and circumstances.

## Task Success and Activity Continuation

Retrieving the ball and continuing to play are distinct outcomes. A failed
retrieval can coexist with continued engagement, just as conversation can
survive awkward phrasing or dancing can survive a missed step.

People may find this forgiving structure carefree or enjoyable. Broad action
opportunities and low costs of mistakes offer a possible interpretation of
that feeling; they do not establish its physical mechanism or tell us what
the dog feels.

The lesson is limited: local task failure need not end a continuing activity.
Social persistence alone does not establish IER's closure, ownership,
membership, or subject-continuity conditions.
