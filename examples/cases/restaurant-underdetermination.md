# Restaurant Underdetermination

## Shared Event, Divergent Anticipated Futures

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.11.6)**
*Illustrative Example · Non-Normative · Non-Criterial*

## Status, Scope, and Authority

This worked interpretation illustrates how observable behavior underdetermines
participant-relative concerns and expectations. It introduces no new IER
criteria and offers no diagnosis of particular people or their experiential
organization. Canonical documents govern the theory.

## One Departure, Several Possible Interpretations

During dinner, a person stands up, appears tense, and leaves without
explanation. Others can agree about the visible departure while responding
differently to it.

| Participant | Possible concern | Possible next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spouse | A disagreement may have caused distress. | Follow, ask what happened, or wait for a quieter moment. |
| Physician | The person may be unwell. | Check on them and ask about symptoms. |
| Child | Something unsettling may be happening. | Seek reassurance or watch quietly. |
| Waiter | The guest may be dissatisfied with the service. | Check the table or offer assistance. |

These are illustrative possibilities, not predictions based on someone's
role. Any participant might respond differently, and the departure might have
an unrelated explanation. The visible action alone does not establish what
anyone actually thinks, feels, or intends.

## What Differs?

A *situation*, in ordinary terms here, is the event as organized for a
participant: what matters, how it is understood, and what can be done next.
An *affordance* is an opportunity for action, such as following the person or
asking a question. *Anticipation* concerns what someone expects or prepares for.

Prior history, knowledge, responsibilities, and current concerns can make
different possibilities salient. Agreement about who left and when therefore
need not produce agreement about what to do next.

Those anticipated possibilities are not a measurement of physically admissible
successors. A person's expectations may be mistaken; describing a concern does
not identify the physical organization that realizes it or establish its UEF
boundary.

## Shared Reality and the Limits of Observation

The participants encounter one physical event. Different interpretations do
not require separate realities, irrationality, or disagreement about the
observed movement.

The case illustrates [Non-Entailment](../../IER/IER-nonentailment.md): an
external description of behavior does not uniquely establish experiential
organization. It also cautions against moving directly from an observed action
to claims about a subject's internal continuation structure.

The shared departure leaves room for different concerns, anticipated futures,
and practical responses. Further observation or conversation may clarify them;
the departure by itself does not settle them.
