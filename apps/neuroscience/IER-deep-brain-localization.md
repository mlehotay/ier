# If Consciousness Is in the Deep Brain, What Exactly Have We Located?

## Causal Localization, Subject Individuation, and the Bearer Problem

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.11.3)**
*Research Note · Non-Normative · External-Literature Bridge · Canon-Constrained*

## Status, Scope, and Authority

This article is a research-facing application of Informational Experiential Realism to contemporary consciousness neuroscience.

It introduces:

* no new ontological primitives;
* no experiential criteria or diagnostic procedures;
* no anatomical claims about where human experience is instantiated;
* no revision to IER subjecthood, individuation, participation, or multiscale commitments;
* no inference from neural correlation, behavioral responsiveness, or perturbational effect to experiential status.

All IER commitments remain fixed by the canonical corpus.

The scientific claims in this article are grounded in peer-reviewed literature. Popular-science discussions may motivate the question, but they do not provide the evidential basis for the argument.

The central issue is narrow:

> **If neuroscience identifies neural structures that are indispensable for consciousness, what exactly has it located?**

IER's answer is that several physically different relations must first be distinguished.

A structure may:

* causally influence experience;
* enable a wider experiential regime;
* modulate experiential content;
* participate constitutively in that regime;
* provide a critical coupling pathway;
* or help determine the physical boundary of the subject.

These are not equivalent discoveries.

## The Localization Question Contains Several Different Questions

Talk of “locating consciousness in the brain” can refer to at least four different problems.

### Phenomenal self-location

Where does the person experience themselves as being?

This is the problem studied through bodily-self illusions, out-of-body phenomena, virtual reality, and multisensory self-location experiments.

It concerns an organization **within experience**.

It does not tell us where the physical system constituting that experience begins or ends.

### Neural causal localization

Which neural structures are causally necessary for wakefulness, conscious perception, or particular conscious contents?

Lesions, stimulation, anesthesia, electrophysiology, neuroimaging, and focused ultrasound can constrain this question.

This identifies causal organization.

### Constitutive localization

Which neural processes are not merely causes or enabling conditions but belong to the physical process that is itself conscious?

This is already a stronger metaphysical question.

### Subject individuation

Which physically extended process constitutes **one** experiential subject rather than zero, two, or several?

This is the question closest to the IER bearer problem.

IER is principally concerned with the last distinction.

The question is not:

> Where inside the brain is the little thing that experiences everything?

IER rejects that picture.

The relevant question is:

> **What physical system, extended through space and time, constitutes one experiential subject?**

## The Current Science Does Not Point to a Neural “Throne Room”

Recent consciousness research does provide increasingly strong evidence that subcortical structures play important causal roles.

But that evidence does not support a simple replacement of corticocentrism with a deep-brain localization theory.

The strongest contemporary picture is increasingly **networked**.

### The 2025 IIT–GNWT adversarial collaboration

The large 2025 adversarial collaboration between Integrated Information Theory and Global Neuronal Workspace Theory did not test “cortex versus subcortex.”

It tested preregistered predictions from IIT and GNWT while 256 participants viewed visual stimuli during fMRI, MEG, or intracranial EEG. Conscious-content information was found in visual, ventrotemporal, and inferior frontal cortex. Some predictions from both theories were supported, while key predictions of both were challenged.

The experiment is therefore important evidence about cortical theories of conscious content.

It does **not** establish that cortex alone constitutes consciousness.

It also does not establish that the failure of either theory would favor a deep-brain subject.

Those are additional inferences.

### Human thalamic intervention

Deep-brain evidence has nevertheless become substantially stronger.

Jang and colleagues used low-intensity focused ultrasound to perturb different thalamic regions during a near-threshold visual-perception task. High-duty-cycle stimulation of ventroanterior thalamus increased object-recognition sensitivity. The same region exhibited strong functional connectivity with frontoparietal and default-mode cortical networks.

The scientifically important result is causal:

> altering a particular thalamic region changed conscious visual perception.

But the network result matters equally.

The finding is not naturally summarized as:

> consciousness is located in ventroanterior thalamus.

The stimulated thalamic region participates in extensive thalamocortical organization.

Fang and colleagues likewise reported direct human evidence that high-order thalamic nuclei participate in conscious perception through thalamofrontal interactions.

Again, this points toward **organized interaction**, not a standalone subcortical container.

### Arousal is itself networked

Edlow and colleagues mapped a proposed human ascending arousal network using high-resolution ex vivo MRI, immunohistochemistry, and connectivity analysis. The identified system includes nodes in brainstem, hypothalamus, thalamus, and basal forebrain and connects extensively with cortical networks associated with awareness.

Likewise, a 2025 study of deep-brain stimulation in disorders of consciousness found that better outcomes were associated not merely with stimulation of a point in the thalamus but with engagement of a wider network including thalamic, brainstem, hypothalamic, and frontal pathways.

The emerging scientific picture is therefore not well described as:

```text
cortex
  versus
deep brain
```

It is closer to:

```text
distributed cortical processing
          ↕
thalamocortical coordination
          ↕
subcortical arousal and regulatory systems
          ↕
brainstem / hypothalamic organization
```

Different structures may perform different necessary roles within one coupled system.

That is exactly why anatomical localization does not settle subject individuation.

## The Historical Subcortical Challenge Is Real

The argument that an intact cortex may not be necessary for all consciousness did not begin with recent focused-ultrasound research.

Björn Merker's 2007 target article, *Consciousness without a cerebral cortex: A challenge for neuroscience and medicine*, argued for a major role of subcortical structures and drew attention to evidence from children with profound congenital cortical loss.

Hydranencephaly is especially provocative.

Shewmon and colleagues described children with congenital absence of most cerebral cortex whose observed behavior they interpreted as inconsistent with an inevitable permanent vegetative condition.

Aleman and Merker later surveyed caregivers of children with hydranencephaly for behavioral evidence **indirectly informative** about conscious status.

That qualifier matters.

These cases can legitimately challenge a strong inference such as:

> no substantial cortex, therefore no possible experience.

They do not establish:

> observable responsiveness proves consciousness.

Nor do they establish:

> the remaining deep brain is therefore the complete conscious subject.

Hydranencephaly involves congenital development, variable residual tissue, substantial neuroplasticity, indirect behavioral evidence, and severe limitations on report.

The scientifically responsible conclusion is therefore narrower:

> **Hydranencephaly makes simple cortical-necessity claims substantially harder to defend, but it does not anatomically locate the bearer of experience.**

IER is particularly well positioned to preserve this distinction because it rejects both anatomical exclusion and behavioral certification.

## Necessary Is Not Constitutive

This distinction is the central philosophical correction.

Suppose future experiments identify a small neural structure such that:

* destroying it reliably abolishes wakeful responsiveness;
* temporarily suppressing it disrupts conscious perception;
* stimulating it restores behavioral signs associated with consciousness;
* its activity predicts transitions between conscious and unconscious conditions.

That would establish profound causal importance.

It would not yet establish that the structure **is the subject**.

Several physical relations remain possible.

The structure might be:

1. **constitutive**
   Its operation is part of the physical regime identical with experience.

2. **enabling**
   It maintains background conditions required for a wider experiential system.

3. **modulatory**
   It reorganizes the contents or stability of an already-existing experiential regime.

4. **a coupling hub**
   Its removal fragments interactions among other constitutive processes.

5. **a bottleneck**
   Many processes depend on it even though the relevant system extends beyond it.

6. **a boundary-relevant participant**
   Its coupling relations may help determine whether a larger candidate system can function as one integrated whole.

These possibilities cannot be collapsed into the statement:

> This is where consciousness lives.

The distinction is familiar elsewhere in biology.

A sinoatrial node can be indispensable for normal cardiac rhythm without the heartbeat being spatially contained inside the node.

A keystone bridge can be indispensable to a transport network without the network being identical with the bridge.

Likewise, destroying a neural hub could abolish an experiential regime because a wider system can no longer sustain itself.

Causal indispensability is therefore evidence about **organization**.

It is not automatically evidence about **ontological extent**.

## Is “Where Is the Bearer?” a Category Error?

There is a serious objection to the entire formulation.

A critic might say:

> Consciousness is a neural or physical process. Why posit a further “bearer” of that process? Searching for a bearer risks recreating a Cartesian subject inside the brain.

IER accepts the force of this objection against one interpretation of the word *bearer*.

There is no IER entity with the structure:

```text
physical brain process
        ↓
experience
        ↓
subject that receives experience
```

IER explicitly rejects such an architecture.

A Unified Experiential Field is not a self-entity, observer, container, representational store, or separate domain. Experience is identical to the lawful operation of the UEF itself.

So if “bearer” means:

> an additional thing behind experience that possesses or watches it,

then IER agrees:

> **there is no bearer to locate.**

But that does not eliminate the individuation problem.

It clarifies it.

Under IER, *bearer* is shorthand for:

> **the physical system whose continuing operation constitutes one experiential subject.**

The bearer is not another component of the ontology.

It is the UEF considered under the question of **numerical identity and physical boundary**.

The correct relation is therefore:

```text
UEF operation
      =
experience
      =
one experiential subject
```

not:

```text
UEF → experience → subject
```

The subject does not stand behind the process.

The subject **is the individuated process**.

## Why the Individuation Problem Does Not Disappear

A process theory cannot avoid individuation merely by rejecting substances.

The question immediately becomes:

> Which process?

Suppose two neural populations communicate.

Are they:

* two causally interacting processes;
* two participating portions of one larger process;
* two experiential subjects;
* one experiential subject with partially segregated processing;
* or non-experiential components of a still larger experiential regime?

Calling consciousness a process does not answer these questions.

It merely moves the problem from substance individuation to process individuation.

IER therefore asks:

> **Where is intrinsic constraint borne as one non-factorizable, temporally continuous physical regime?**

IER's closure relation is explicitly structural rather than anatomical, and ownership asks where irreversible traversal is borne.

Informational slack supplies an exclusion principle. If constraint can remain confined to a proper subsystem without reorganizing the larger candidate system, then the larger candidate does not possess the globally binding closure required of a UEF.

That does not provide an observational consciousness detector.

It provides the structure of the individuation problem.

## Split-Brain Cases Show Why Individuation Matters

Split-brain research makes this issue unusually concrete.

The classical intuition was straightforward:

```text
one connected brain
      ↓ callosotomy
two disconnected hemispheres
      ↓
two conscious subjects
```

But the empirical situation is substantially more difficult.

Pinto and colleagues reported experimental findings they interpreted as showing divided visual perception without division into two independent conscious perceivers. Their later theoretical treatment proposed a “conscious unity, split perception” model in which one conscious agent possesses two parallel, partially unintegrated streams of information.

That interpretation remains debated.

Its importance here is methodological.

Severing a major information pathway does not make the number of subjects transparent.

The relevant question is not merely:

> How much information crosses the corpus callosum?

It is:

> **After the intervention, what physical organization continues to function as one subject-bearing system?**

That is exactly the problem IER means by individuation.

## Hemispherotomy Provides an Even Stronger Boundary Perturbation

Hemispherotomy creates an especially informative natural experiment.

In the procedure, extensive cortical tissue—sometimes essentially an entire cerebral hemisphere—is disconnected from cortical and subcortical communication while remaining living tissue.

Colombo and colleagues examined ten pediatric patients before and after such disconnection. The isolated cortex developed prominent slow oscillations and broadband spectral properties resembling deep NREM sleep rather than the waking state of the connected hemisphere. The authors explicitly state that inaccessible awareness in the disconnected cortex remains difficult to establish; their findings only provisionally support absent or reduced awareness.

This distinction is essential.

The experiment does not prove:

> the disconnected hemisphere is not conscious.

Nor does it prove:

> cortex requires subcortex because subcortex contains consciousness.

What it demonstrates directly is that large-scale disconnection profoundly alters the dynamical regime sustained by the isolated cortex. The authors also emphasize the importance of thalamocortical circuits in organizing cortical dynamics.

For IER, this is exactly the kind of experiment that matters.

Not because a spectral marker can certify subjecthood.

But because surgical partition reveals what organization a physical subsystem can and cannot sustain after a major route of coupling is removed.

## IIT Shows That the Boundary Problem Is Already a Scientific Problem

Integrated Information Theory is especially relevant to IER even where their ontologies differ.

IIT 4.0 explicitly recognizes that a theory of consciousness must determine a **definite border and grain** for the physical substrate of an experience. Its exclusion principle and associated formalism are intended to identify which interacting units constitute one conscious complex rather than competing overlapping candidates.

That makes IIT an important neighboring research programme.

The significance for IER is not that IER should inherit $\Phi$, IIT's phenomenological axioms, or its diagnostic ambitions.

It should not.

The important observation is methodological:

> **A physical theory of one subject cannot leave system individuation arbitrary.**

IIT has constructed one explicit solution to that problem.

IER proposes a different basis:

* globally intrinsic constraint;
* non-factorizability;
* non-externalizable ownership;
* absence of relevant informational slack;
* temporal continuity of one regime.

IER therefore has a genuine formal research burden parallel to, but distinct from, IIT's exclusion problem:

> Given nested, overlapping, or alternative candidate partitions of one physical system, which candidate—if any—constitutes a UEF?

That question survives regardless of whether the biological answer turns out to be cortical, subcortical, thalamocortical, whole-brain, organism-level, or organized at some unexpected physical scale.

## Cortex Versus Subcortex May Be the Wrong Ontological Binary

Anatomical categories are scientifically useful.

They need not coincide with experiential boundaries.

The cortex is an anatomical class.

The thalamus is an anatomical class.

The brainstem is an anatomical class.

A UEF, under IER, is a **regime boundary**.

IER therefore does not infer:

```text
anatomical boundary
        =
experiential boundary
```

A constitutive physical organization could cross conventional anatomical divisions.

Conversely, neighboring neural tissue need not all belong to the same constitutive organization merely because it is spatially adjacent.

This is one reason the deep-brain debate should not be framed as a competition for the location of an internal throne room.

The scientifically relevant alternatives may instead be:

* cortex-dependent distributed organization;
* subcortically enabled cortical organization;
* thalamocortical recurrent organization;
* cortico-subcortical network organization;
* wider brain–body organization;
* or some still-undetermined regime boundary.

IER cannot select among these possibilities from theory alone.

Their existence is contingent neuroscience.

The theory contributes only a structural warning:

> **Do not mistake the anatomical level at which a system is described for the level at which a subject is individuated.**

## Participation Is Not Subjecthood

This distinction also prevents an important mistake about cortical content.

Suppose deep-brain structures turn out to be indispensable to maintaining the relevant human UEF.

It would not follow that cortex is therefore non-experiential.

IER distinguishes **participation** from **subjecthood**.

Processes can participate in one UEF without themselves constituting additional subjects.

A cortical process might:

* alter the UEF's qualitative organization;
* change salience;
* reorganize admissible futures;
* supply perceptual structure;
* modify memory, reasoning, language, or abstraction;

while the subject remains the larger UEF to which that process belongs.

Conversely, a structure can causally affect the UEF from outside its constitutive boundary.

Determining which relation applies is the individuation problem.

The same distinction prevents the inverse mistake:

> If the thalamus is indispensable, the thalamus must itself be the subject.

Indispensability establishes neither separate subjecthood nor complete subjecthood.

## Focused Ultrasound Is Valuable Precisely Because It Does Not Settle the Ontology

Focused ultrasound is important because it improves the precision with which deep neural structures can be causally perturbed.

The 2025 thalamic study demonstrates the basic promise: region-specific intervention changed measurable conscious visual perception in humans.

That makes focused ultrasound valuable to an IER research programme.

It may help identify:

* causal dependencies;
* coupling pathways;
* transition-sensitive nodes;
* propagation across cortical and subcortical systems;
* structures necessary for maintaining regime stability;
* consequences of temporarily weakening particular interactions.

What it cannot do alone is identify which philosophical relation the altered structure has to experience.

The experimental result:

> perturbation of A changes conscious process B

does not entail:

> A is the subject.

Nor does it entail:

> A is outside the subject.

Those conclusions require analysis of the larger organization.

## What an IER-Aligned Experimental Programme Would Ask

IER should therefore not approach neuroscience by asking researchers to “find the UEF.”

That wording would invite exactly the diagnostic inference the theory rejects.

A more disciplined research programme would ask how candidate physical boundaries behave under intervention.

For a proposed system $\Sigma$ and a candidate partition into subsystems, the useful questions include:

### Can relevant constraint remain local?

If perturbation of one subsystem can be absorbed and resolved there without reorganizing the larger candidate system, that is evidence of independence across the proposed partition.

Under IER, genuine localizability is incompatible with treating the larger system as one globally intrinsic UEF.

### Does resolution propagate globally?

If locally introduced perturbations necessarily alter the admissible continuation of the entire candidate system, this constrains decomposition hypotheses.

It still does not prove experience.

### What survives physical disconnection?

Callosotomy and hemispherotomy are especially valuable because they alter the physical topology itself rather than merely measuring correlation.

### Which dynamics are enabling and which are participating?

A node that maintains wakefulness may have a different relation to the experiential regime than a process whose changing organization tracks conscious content.

### Does the same regime persist through time?

IER subjecthood is not a momentary connectivity relation.

Experience exists across non-zero duration, and identity concerns continued ownership of the same history–future frontier.

These are all ordinary physical questions.

What IER blocks is the final illicit move:

> We measured integration, therefore we measured experience.

The framework does not license it.

## Why “Locating the Bearer” Still Matters

The phrase can now be rehabilitated carefully.

To locate the bearer is **not**:

* to locate an inner witness;
* to find the point from which the brain is observed;
* to locate the phenomenological center of the head;
* to identify whichever structure most strongly affects consciousness;
* to identify a neural correlate;
* to identify a consciousness switch.

It is:

> **to individuate the physical extent and temporal continuity of the process whose operation constitutes one subject.**

That is a legitimate scientific and philosophical question.

Indeed, any physical theory that allows more than one conscious subject in reality eventually encounters it.

Where does one subject end?

When does one become two?

Can a disconnected subsystem sustain another subject?

Can a new component become constitutively incorporated?

Can two candidate subjects overlap physically?

Which scale governs individuation when several organizational scales coexist?

These are not Cartesian questions.

They are questions about numerical identity among physical processes.

## What Deep-Brain Research Could Eventually Discover

Several outcomes remain open.

### Deep structures could be primarily enabling

Brainstem and thalamic systems might maintain arousal and coordination while the constitutive organization of experience lies primarily elsewhere.

### Deep structures could be constitutive participants

Thalamic, hypothalamic, brainstem, or basal forebrain processes might belong directly to the physical regime constituting the subject.

### A distributed cortico-subcortical regime could be constitutive

No compact anatomical region may be privileged. Experience could depend on organization spanning multiple anatomical systems.

### A smaller subcortical regime could constitute the subject while cortical processes dynamically participate

This is a logically coherent possibility under IER.

But current evidence does not establish it.

### Conventional neuroanatomical categories could fail to match the relevant boundary

The eventual physical individuation may cut across familiar divisions altogether.

IER should leave every one of these possibilities open until physical evidence constrains them.

## The Strongest Current Conclusion

Current neuroscience justifies a significant correction to a strongly cortex-centered picture of consciousness.

Subcortical systems are not merely inert power supplies beneath a conscious cortex.

Human thalamic intervention changes conscious perception.

Human recordings implicate high-order thalamic interactions in conscious perception.

Brainstem, hypothalamic, thalamic, basal-forebrain, and cortical systems form interconnected arousal and awareness networks.

Hydranencephaly prevents easy identification of extensive cortex with a necessary condition for every possible experience.

Split-brain and hemispherotomy research show that physical disconnection creates difficult questions about experiential unity and subsystem independence rather than automatically revealing the number of conscious subjects.

But none of these results presently identifies a complete physical bearer.

That stronger task requires subject individuation.

## The More Radical Possibility

The deep-brain debate is often posed as:

> What if consciousness is generated in evolutionarily ancient structures rather than cortex?

IER proposes a different reframing.

Perhaps consciousness is not **generated** anywhere.

Not because it is immaterial.

Not because neuroscience cannot study it.

Not because there is a non-physical subject outside the brain.

But because generation may be the wrong relation.

The physical system does not first operate and then manufacture a second phenomenal product.

Under the IER identity claim:

> **the operation of the correctly individuated physical regime is the experience.**

There is likewise no additional bearer standing behind that operation.

The bearer is simply the same physical regime considered under the question:

> **Which one is it?**

If future neuroscience succeeds in identifying the physical organization that constitutes one human experiential subject, the discovery will therefore be deeper than finding a consciousness center.

It will not amount to:

> Here is the neural machinery that sends experience to the subject.

Nor merely:

> Here is the structure without which consciousness disappears.

The stronger result would be:

> **Here is the physical process whose continuing operation constitutes this subject.**

That is what it would mean to locate the bearer.
