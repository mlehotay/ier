# *The Edible Woman* / IER Claims Ledger

## Status and purpose

This is a working evidence and argument ledger for a non-canonical IER bridge article on Margaret Atwood's *The Edible Woman*. It does not modify IER canon and does not treat the novel as evidence for IER's truth.

The ledger separates four things that the finished article must not collapse:

1. what the novel explicitly depicts;
2. what Marian or another character says or concludes;
3. what a literary interpretation reasonably infers;
4. what IER offers as an explanatory redescription.

The novel remains the authority for wording, sequence, and characterization. Chapter references are used here because pagination varies by edition. Before publication, every direct quotation and page number must be checked against the edition cited in the article.

## Deliberate scope boundary

This is not a comprehensive plot ledger and should not become a map of every variable in Marian's life. A scene belongs here only when it directly clarifies at least one of the article's controlling problems:

1. the formation and generalization of Marian's food refusals;
2. the extension of refusal from eating to killing or removing mold;
3. the possibility of severe experiential crisis while surrounding people and practices continue to classify the situation as normal;
4. the consumer/consumed or user/used relation that Marian encounters with Peter and Duncan;
5. the camera, cake, disengagement, and qualified return of appetite.

Secondary-character plots, institutional details, and other literary contexts remain relevant to the novel but are outside this ledger unless they materially change that trajectory.

# I. Controlling textual chronology

This is the sequence that should govern every later compression in the article.

| Stage | Chapter(s) | Controlling textual claim | Function in the argument |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rice-pudding baseline | 2, 4 | Marian tastes canned rice pudding while working on a market-research questionnaire; Clara later serves the same time-saving product at home. | Establishes the product as ordinary artificial food distributed through work and domestic life before it becomes either refuge or threat. |
| Early bodily instability | 6 | Marian dreams that her feet are dissolving into jelly and her fingers are becoming transparent. | Shows that disturbance of bodily and personal integrity precedes the proposal and categorical food refusal. |
| Pre-engagement disturbance | 8 | Peter's graphic rabbit-hunting story makes Marian cry without initially recognizing that she is crying. She later runs away. | Establishes vulnerability, hunting, involuntary response, and flight before the engagement. |
| Flight, hiding, proposal | 9–10 | Marian runs, is pursued by Peter, hides under Len's bed, attempts to leave again, and is followed. Peter raises marriage that night; the engagement is reaffirmed the following day. | Shows that bodily and behavioural refusal precede direct assent. |
| Delegated decisions | 10 | Marian tells Peter that he should make the “big decisions,” while remaining uncertain about what she has said. | Strongest textual anchor for displaced or externalized trajectory authorship. |
| First-person compression | 12 | Part One closes with Marian organizing herself around tasks and ratifying Peter as a sensible choice. | Provides the immediate formal threshold before the narrative-person shift. |
| Third-person beginning | 13 | Part Two begins in third-person narration focused on Marian. Her appetite is already becoming unreliable. | Voice shift follows engagement but precedes categorical food refusal. |
| Duncan's explicit substitution | 16 | Duncan tells Marian that their contact means nothing to him and calls her another substitute for the laundromat. | Establishes that the apparent alternative to Peter also organizes Marian instrumentally. |
| Animal flesh | 17–18 | Steak becomes perceptually organized as flesh, blood, cow, butcher, and violence. The aversion generalizes to beef, pork, and mutton; fish remains possible at this stage. | The ordinary food affordance is displaced by a harm/destruction organization. |
| Egg | 18 | Len recounts finding a developed chick inside an egg. The next morning an egg appears frightening and alive to Marian. | Possible/developing life becomes sufficient to make the food unavailable. |
| Artificial refuge | 19–20 | Marian can eat jelly and desserts at the office party and treats canned rice pudding as safe because it seems artificial. | Shows that artificiality temporarily blocks the living-organism interpretation. |
| Plant | 20 | A carrot becomes a living root that may have screamed when pulled from the ground and may still be alive. | The refusal expands beyond animal categories. |
| Ethical explanation | 20–21 | Marian retrospectively reasons that her body has taken an ethical stand against eating what was or might still be living. | Explicit interpretation follows behavioural reorganization. |
| Instrumental relief with Duncan | 21–22 | Marian recognizes that Duncan uses her as an emotional outlet but finds relief in his low expectations. He proposes sex primarily to satisfy his own curiosity, and Marian temporarily removes her engagement ring before later replacing it. | Duncan offers relief from one performance without providing non-instrumental relation or release from the engagement. |
| Social concealment | 22 | At Trevor's dinner Marian eats shrimp and soup but secretly transfers the meat course to Duncan. | The aversion reorganizes social action and concealment, not merely private appetite. |
| Normalization and processed-food failure | 23 | Clara attributes Marian's eating crisis to wedding nerves; Peter tells her she is normal. Meanwhile rice-pudding globules become cocoons and sponge cake becomes thousands of lungs. | The crisis becomes more disabling while trusted people continue to classify the surrounding future and Marian herself as ordinary. |
| Mold and cleaning | 25 | After eating nothing all day and taking a vitamin, Marian hesitates to clean because the mold may have as much right to life as she does. | The ethical/life organization spreads from eating to disposal and cleaning. Mold is not a food refusal. |
| Manufactured party self | 24–27 | Peter suggests a new dress and elaborate hair; salon work and Ainsley's styling leave Marian artificial and unrecognizable to herself. | The social role becomes visually and materially staged. |
| Camera refusal | 26–27 | An attempted posed photograph is interrupted; later Marian screams when Peter points the camera at her and flees while imagining him as a marksman with a lethal weapon. | Recombines hunter/prey, manufactured appearance, and resistance to image capture. |
| Failed detour | 28–29 | Marian attempts sex and escape with Duncan, but the encounter does not resolve the crisis. The next morning she cannot consume toast or orange juice, fears starving, and discovers that Duncan will provide neither safety nor intervention with Peter. | Changing partner and activity does not disengage Marian from the governing crisis; Duncan is not a rescuer or alternative author of her future. |
| Deliberate construction | 30 | Marian buys new ingredients, bakes without a recipe, cuts and decorates the cake as a woman in a pink party dress with green eyes and party-like hair. | First sustained constructive action that externalizes the disputed relation. |
| Explicit substitute | 30 | Marian accuses Peter of trying to destroy or assimilate her and explicitly calls the cake a substitute. | The language of substitution is primary-text evidence, not merely critical metaphor. |
| Direct refusal and appetite | 30 | Peter leaves without eating the cake. Marian becomes ravenous and begins eating it herself. | Direct confrontation replaces displaced food refusal; appetite returns immediately, though its meaning remains interpretive. |
| First-person return | 31 | The next day first-person narration returns. Marian cleans the apartment, says she has left Peter, reports eating steak, and gives the remaining cake to Duncan. | Strong textual cluster supporting reorganization, without proving complete liberation. |
| Causal and ethical ambiguity | 31 | Duncan wonders whether Peter was trying to destroy Marian or Marian was trying to destroy Peter, then calls Marian a consumer and eats the cake's face and hair. | Blocks singular causal blame and a purity narrative: consumption continues and no final ethical innocence is achieved. |

# VI. Central thesis candidates

## Short thesis

> *The Edible Woman* can be read as a study of profound crisis inside an outwardly normal continuation and of refusal displaced across behavioural domains. Bodily instability and flight precede Marian's explicit account of what is wrong, while Clara and Peter continue to classify her condition as nerves or normality. As the consumer/consumed relation becomes experience-dominant, food ceases to afford nourishment and increasingly recruits livingness, destruction, and self-objectification; Duncan's frank use of Marian provides temporary relief but no exit from that organization. The woman-cake, explicitly offered as a substitute, gives the relation a material model through which Marian can confront Peter, disengage from the engagement trajectory, and reorganize appetite and first-person narration without achieving ethical purity or a complete causal explanation.

## Stronger IER thesis

> Marian's crisis does not require a second bodily subject that knows what her conscious mind cannot admit, nor does its invisibility to others make it unreal. It can be described as a trajectory in which an ordinary, socially intelligible future coexists with early bodily destabilization; autonomic constraint, salience, interpretation, and affordances then make consumption progressively unavailable before an explicit explanation stabilizes. Because changing food behaviour, removing a ring, or seeking Duncan does not release the governing organization—and because Duncan himself reproduces instrumental use—refusal narrows toward starvation without becoming direct disengagement. The cake changes the situation by materially preserving the disputed relation—woman made attractive, available, and edible—while substituting an object Marian has authored for the subject who must live the future. The ending restores reorganizability and direct authorship, not innocence, a metaphysical self, ethical closure, or certainty that Peter alone caused the crisis.

## Most cautious formulation of the bridge hypothesis

> The article proposes—not as a claim of authorial intention or currently stabilized IER canon—that Marian's food refusal preserves and reverses the central relation organizing her situation: when refusal of being made consumable or usable is not yet stably available, refusal appears as an inability to consume. Peter and Duncan instantiate materially different versions of that relation; Duncan's failure tests whether changing companions is enough to change the organization. The cake makes the proposed topology explicit enough to test against the novel's language of hunting, photography, use, assimilation, substitution, eating, and the return of the first person.

# VII. Drafting rules for the full article

1. Attribute claims at the correct level:
   - “the novel depicts” for events;
   - “Marian concludes” for the ethical rule and accusation;
   - “the sequence supports” for literary inference;
   - “IER can describe” for theoretical redescription;
   - “this article proposes” for behavioural substitution.
2. Use the exact chronology. Never place mold before processed food.
3. Introduce the Chapter 6 dissolution dream before the rabbit, running, and hiding so the proposal and steak do not appear to create disturbance from nothing.
4. Keep appetite disturbance, categorical food refusal, narration shift, and the later ethical interpretation temporally distinct.
5. Use Clara's “wedding nerves” and Peter's “normal” response to establish mismatch between lived crisis and ordinary recognition. Do not treat their reassurance as evidence that nothing serious is happening.
6. Treat Duncan as a necessary failed alternative: he openly uses Marian, provides relief from performance, and does not release her from the governing crisis. Do not turn him into either rescuer or second primary villain.
7. Preserve the full canned-rice-pudding trajectory from market-research product to domestic convenience, artificial refuge, and cocoons.
8. Do not literalize narrative person as an ontological or diagnostic state.
9. Do not give Marian's body independent agency, knowledge, or moral authority.
10. Do not infer IER standing from biological life or Marian's empathy.
11. Treat `IER-consent.md`, `IER-coercion.md`, and `IER-capture.md` as qualified secondary lenses. The primary social anchors are culture, social stack, trajectory authorship, situation dominance, and narrowing.
12. Treat `IER-constraint-dominance.md` as a boundary article, not the primary explanation.
13. Mark behavioural substitution as a local bridge hypothesis until a canonical article stabilizes the term.
14. Describe the cake as both:
    - an ordinary literary representation, and
    - an IER model that preserves organization for bounded consequence projection.
    Do not treat representation as an IER ontological primitive.
15. End with Duncan's reversal of the destruction question and his “consumer” complication. The article should resolve displacement without pretending to resolve causality or consumption.
16. Enforce the scope boundary: add secondary plots only when they directly change the food, mold, normality, instrumental-use, camera, cake, or ending trajectory.
