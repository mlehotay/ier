# Becoming Inedible

## Food Refusal, Normality, and Situation Authorship in *The Edible Woman*

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.11.3)**  
*Tier 4 · Literary Bridge · Draft · Non-Normative · Non-Canonical · Canon-Constrained*

## Status, Scope, and Authority

This article applies Informational Experiential Realism to Margaret Atwood's *The Edible Woman*. It treats the novel as a fictional narrative whose organization can be redescribed through IER. It does not treat the novel as evidence for IER, as a source of canonical theory, or as a report from which psychological or clinical conclusions may be drawn.

This article introduces no ontological primitive, diagnostic, ethical rule, subjecthood criterion, or causal mechanism. Its use of **behavioural substitution** is a local bridge hypothesis developed through the novel. The term is not currently a stabilized part of IER canon.

The analysis also distinguishes among:

- events the novel depicts;
- explanations Marian or another character gives;
- literary inferences supported by the narrative structure;
- IER redescriptions of those inferences.

These levels must not be collapsed. Marian's interpretation of her food refusal is not automatically the novel's final judgement, and the novel's imagery is not automatically an IER mechanism.

The article is deliberately narrower than the novel. It does not attempt to map every relationship, institution, or secondary plot in Marian's life. It follows only the events needed to understand her expanding food refusals, her refusal to kill or remove mould, the persistence of severe crisis inside an outwardly normal situation, Duncan's instrumental use of her, the camera, the cake, and the qualified return of appetite. Other events remain important to *The Edible Woman* without belonging to this argument.

All theoretical authority remains with the canonical IER corpus. If this bridge article conflicts with a governing source, that source prevails.

## Abstract

*The Edible Woman* can be read as a study of profound crisis inside an outwardly normal continuation and of refusal displaced across behavioural domains. Before Marian MacAlpin refuses steak, accepts Peter's proposal, or offers an ethical explanation for her eating, she dreams that her feet are dissolving and her fingers becoming transparent. She later cries at Peter's rabbit-hunting story without initially recognizing her tears, runs, hides, delegates the “big decisions,” and enters a future that remains socially intelligible while becoming experientially unlivable. When Marian eventually seeks confirmation, Clara calls the eating problem wedding nerves and Peter tells her that she is normal. Their reassurance does not resolve the crisis; it reveals a mismatch between Marian's action field and the interpretations available around her.

Under an IER reading, Marian's eating problem does not require a second bodily subject communicating a truth to her conscious self. Behaviour, bodily regulation, salience, interpretation, and affordances can reorganize within one experiential subject before an explicit account stabilizes. The food sequence makes that reorganization unusually visible. Canned rice pudding moves from market-research sample, to domestic convenience, to apparently safe artificial food, and finally to imagined cocoons. Meat becomes slaughtered flesh, an egg possible life, a carrot an uprooted organism, processed foods organs or latent creatures, and cleaning an act that would destroy mould. Food increasingly ceases to afford nourishment and instead affords ethical conflict, disgust, concealment, or refusal.

Duncan supplies the missing test of the trajectory. He differs sharply from Peter and relieves Marian of the demand to perform conventional affection, yet he openly treats her as a substitute for the laundromat, an emotional outlet, and a low-obligation sexual possibility. Marian can find relief in not minding that she is being used, remove and replace her engagement ring, flee with Duncan, and attempt sex with him without leaving the governing crisis. The next morning she still cannot eat and discovers that Duncan will provide neither safety nor intervention with Peter. A different companion and activity do not by themselves produce disengagement.

This article therefore proposes the novel-facing hypothesis of **behavioural substitution**: when refusal of being made consumable or usable is not yet stably available, refusal appears as an inability to consume. The proposed substitution preserves and reverses the governing relation while changing its target. The woman-shaped cake then gives that relation a material, self-authored model. By offering Peter an edible substitute for herself, Marian makes direct accusation and refusal available. The engagement ceases to govern continuation, appetite and first-person narration return, and Marian becomes more reorganizable. The ending nevertheless refuses purity and simple causality. Duncan wonders whether Peter was trying to destroy Marian or Marian was trying to destroy Peter, then calls her a consumer. What changes is not Marian's implication in consumption, but her availability for assimilation into a future she has not adequately authored.

## Critical Starting Point

The broad feminist-consumer reading is already well established. Critics have long connected eating and being eaten with power, gendered availability, consumer classification, bodily protest, threatened identity, and the first-person / third-person / first-person form. Emma Parker treats eating as a metaphor for power and non-eating as both powerlessness and protest. J. Brooks Bouson warns against explanations that master the novel too neatly. Jennifer Hobgood distinguishes affirmative liberation readings from materialist-feminist accounts and shows how the novel's form unsettles both. Cailin Flannery Roles places Marian's self-alienation within postwar consumer culture and likewise rejects an easy escape from implication. See [Parker 1995](https://www.jstor.org/stable/441857), [Bouson 1990](https://www.jstor.org/stable/42945853), [Hobgood 2002](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5325/style.36.1.146), and [Roles 2021](https://doi.org/10.7560/TSLL63303).

This article does not claim novelty for saying that Marian fears being consumed, that her body protests, that the cake externalizes consumable femininity, or that the ending is qualified. Its narrower contribution is an IER redescription of how those established relations become action-organizing: how behaviour can reorganize before explanation, how food can stop affording eating, how a crisis can remain severe while ordinary social interpretation reports normality, how relief can coexist with instrumental use, how refusal can transfer across domains while reversing Marian's position, and how a material model can change the situation it models.

# 1. Disturbance Before Refusal

The food refusal is the novel's most visible crisis, but it is not the beginning of Marian's disturbance.

In Chapter 6, before Peter's proposal and before the categorical refusal of any food, Marian dreams that her feet are dissolving into jelly and her fingers are becoming transparent. The dream does not diagnose her, reveal a hidden truth, or prove that one later event causes the whole trajectory. It does establish that bodily and personal integrity are already unstable. Marian's crisis cannot be reduced to a worked-out ethical objection to meat, and the engagement does not create distress in an otherwise untouched subject.

The next major sequence makes that instability behavioural. Before Peter proposes marriage, he tells a graphic story about hunting and killing a rabbit. Marian begins to cry without initially recognizing that she is crying. Later she runs away, is pursued, hides under Len's bed, attempts to leave again, and is followed. The proposal emerges after this sequence of tears, flight, hiding, pursuit, argument, and reconciliation rather than as an isolated conventional scene (Chapters 8–10).

This chronology prevents a simple causal story in which Marian calmly accepts marriage and then develops a symptom that reveals her hidden objection. Her participation is already unstable. Refusal exists as bodily dissolution imagery, involuntary tears, movement, hiding, argument, and attempted escape before it becomes a dietary pattern. The engagement supplies a socially intelligible continuation around which that disturbance can become more tightly organized, but it is not an origin point that explains everything before it.

The strongest textual sign is not the proposal by itself but Marian's delegation of the “big decisions” to Peter. The line does not establish that Marian has no agency. She continues to act, interpret, resist, conceal, improvise, and bear the consequences of what follows. It does show a local externalization of trajectory shaping. A large region of her practical future is being supplied in advance, and she invites Peter to govern its major resolutions while remaining uncertain that the invitation expresses what she wants.

IER locates authorship across a trajectory rather than at a single metaphysical choice point. A verbal yes is therefore neither the whole act nor the whole history. The relevant question is how constraint is redistributed over time: which continuations become easier, which become costly, which roles recruit participation, and whose organization supplies the shape of the future.

Marian's trajectory around the engagement is mixed. She assents, resists, delegates, prepares, flees, and remains engaged. The novel's power lies partly in refusing to compress this mixture into either free choice or pure imposition.

> The engagement is not one decision that explains everything. It is an increasingly organized continuation inside a disturbance that has already begun.

# 2. A Normal Future Around an Unrecognized Crisis

Peter is central to Marian's crisis, but he is not its complete mechanism. Nor does the apparent normality of the engagement show that nothing serious is happening.

The article needs only a narrow account of the environment. At Seymour Surveys, Marian revises survey language, participates in product research, and encounters people through consumer categories. The engagement arrives with an already familiar sequence of proposal, planning, appearance, party, photography, and marriage. Peter does not have to invent the role of future husband, and Marian does not have to invent the role of future wife. Dress, hair, makeup, jewellery, hospitality, and photographs materially prepare a publicly recognizable version of the couple.

These elements are not interchangeable causes, and they do not form a collective mind. Under IER they can be described as a **social stack**: material, institutional, linguistic, relational, and cultural constraints that scaffold particular continuations. Culture lowers the cost of some futures by making them familiar, nameable, repeatable, and socially supported. A conventional future can continue through many small acts without anyone issuing an explicit command or possessing a complete plan.

Marriage is therefore present not only as something Peter wants. It arrives as a ready-made narrative with practical infrastructure. It supplies roles, sequencing, expectations, appearance, and an anticipated continuation. Marian does not need to invent this future for it to recruit her. The future is normal in the precise sense that people around her already know what it is, how it should look, and what should happen next.

That normality becomes most important when Marian tries to determine whether her own condition is abnormal. After even canned rice pudding has become unavailable, she seeks help from Clara. Clara interprets the problem as wedding nerves. Marian also fears that telling Peter about the eating problem could cause him to cancel the wedding. Instead she asks whether she is normal, and he tells her that she is.

Nothing in these exchanges requires Clara or Peter to be deceptive. Their answers can be sincere and still fail to track Marian's condition. Clara has an available explanation for anxiety before marriage. Peter recognizes the fiancée with whom ordinary plans are continuing. Neither response reaches the reorganized action field in which food is becoming inedible, cleaning is about to become killing, and Marian fears starvation.

IER helps state the mismatch without granting either side automatic authority. A situation is not defined solely by its public name. Two people can occupy the same room, relationship, or event while different organizations determine what can happen next for each of them. “Wedding” can afford ordinary continuation for Peter while increasingly failing to afford an inhabitable future for Marian. “Food” can remain plainly edible to everyone around her while no longer affording eating to her. Social recognition and experiential organization can diverge without producing a visible external emergency.

This is the central sense in which Marian can be in crisis while everything remains normal around her:

> Normality is the surrounding classification of the continuation. It is not evidence that the continuation remains livable for the subject who must undergo it.

This does not establish that Peter alone harms Marian or that every conventional relationship hides equivalent crisis. It establishes a narrower structural possibility. Severe narrowing may be externally subtle, behaviourally managed, socially normalized, and interpreted through ordinary explanations while remaining fully real within the subject's action field.

Technical terms such as **capture**, **consent**, and **coercion** are therefore secondary here. Marian's intrinsic experiential ownership does not transfer to Peter or “society.” Her verbal assent matters, but it does not exhaust whether the coupling is integrated across the trajectory. No explicit threat is required for practical momentum and identity cost to favour continuation. The stronger and safer description is that trajectory shaping becomes partially externalized while Marian's local responses become progressively less integrated with the future being prepared.

The primary point is environmental and experiential at once:

> Marian enters a future whose cultural scaffolding is ordinary and whose local continuation is becoming impossible for her.

# 3. The Woman Who Becomes Third Person

The novel formalizes Marian's altered relation to her situation through its narrative voice. Parts One and Three use first-person narration. Part Two, beginning in Chapter 13, refers to Marian in the third person.

The timing is precise. The shift follows the engagement, but it precedes the categorical refusal of steak in Chapter 17. The novel does not simply switch from “I” to “she” at the instant Marian stops eating. Narrative reorganization and food refusal overlap within the same trajectory without beginning as the same event.

This prevents an overly literal interpretation. Marian does not cease to be the experiential subject during Part Two. The third-person form does not establish loss of identity, ownership, consciousness, or agency. The narration remains organized around her experience and action. She continues to interpret, decide, conceal, seek alternatives, and suffer consequences.

IER's distinction between the subject and the self-model is useful here. The subject is not a represented character inside experience. The narrative self is a coordination structure through which histories, roles, expectations, and anticipated continuations can be stabilized. Changes in self-description can reorganize action without creating, destroying, or relocating the subject.

The shift to “she” can therefore be read as a literary staging of changed participation in self-organization. Marian increasingly encounters herself as someone already located within an externally supplied account: employee, fiancée, future wife, party object, photographed woman. The narrative form makes her available for description from within that organization. She is not absent; her participation in describing and authoring the situation has become unstable.

The return of first person in Chapter 31 is likewise not the resurrection of a metaphysical self. It follows the construction of the cake, Marian's confrontation with Peter, his departure, and Marian's first eating of the cake. The formal return supports a reorganization reading because it coincides with changed action, appetite, and trajectory governance. It does not prove complete recovery.

> The subject does not disappear into “she” and return as “I.” What changes is the narrative organization through which Marian participates in her own continuation.

# 4. Refusal Before Explanation

The novel often personifies Marian's body and stomach. They refuse, close, take positions, or seem to change their minds. This divided idiom is central to the comedy and terror of the book, but it should not be turned into a hidden-executive theory.

It is tempting to say that Marian's body knows she is being consumed before Marian consciously understands it. That formulation installs a second knower: a bodily intelligence that discovers the truth and sends symptoms to an otherwise separate self. IER blocks that division.

There is one continuously operating experiential subject. Deliberative, autonomic, perceptual, affective, narrative, and regulatory participation can differ in salience and availability without becoming separate subjects. Marian's nausea, stomach closure, inability to swallow, and loss of appetite are forms of organization within her experience, not messages from another agent.

The sequence supports a narrower and stronger claim:

> Marian's behaviour reorganizes before her explicit interpretation of that behaviour stabilizes.

At dinner with Peter, steak stops being available as ordinary food. Flesh, blood, cow, butcher, and violence become action-organizing. The aversion then generalizes to beef, pork, and mutton. Fish remains possible at this point. After Len describes finding a developed chick in an egg, an egg becomes frightening and alive to Marian. Only later does she formulate the explanation that her body has taken an “ethical stand” against eating what was or might still be living.

The explanation is retrospective. It is a model Marian builds to make an already changing pattern intelligible. Retrospective does not mean false, irrelevant, or merely decorative. An interpretation can stabilize relations, reorganize salience, and recruit later situations. Once Marian has organized the refusal around livingness and destruction, new foods can more readily appear as further cases of the same problem.

This creates a feedback relation:

1. food becomes unavailable before a general rule is stated;
2. Marian builds an ethical model of the refusal;
3. the model helps organize later encounters;
4. later encounters appear to confirm and broaden the model.

The model neither reveals a secret agent nor becomes epistemically privileged because it fits. It is an organization that makes the sequence coherent enough to continue.

## Autonomia Without a Bodily Homunculus

`IER-autonomia.md` describes cases in which autonomic and regulatory participation becomes experientially foregrounded. Cognition and planning are not removed, but their available continuations may be constrained by nausea, digestion, arousal, pain, or viability demand.

This is a productive lens for Marian. Her eating problem is not usually a loss of UEF-level governance. It is an increasingly dominant bodily and regulatory constraint that makes some actions unavailable or costly while experience remains unified. She can understand that food is conventionally edible and still be unable to eat it. She can plan concealment, transfer meat to Duncan, take a vitamin, seek a safe food, and fear starvation. These actions show continuing agency under narrowing, not displacement of agency by another subject.

For the same reason, **constraint dominance** should not be the article's default explanation. In its technical IER use, constraint dominance involves failure of ordinary regime governance under binding overload. Most of Marian's sequence is better described through autonomic foregrounding, salience, interpretation, affordance reorganization, and narrowing. Constraint dominance may be locally suggestive where gagging or comparable bodily thresholds overrun an intended action, but it should not name the whole crisis.

# 5. When Food Stops Affording Food

An affordance is not a meaning stored in an object. It is a locally admissible continuation generated by a system's organization within an environment. Food affords eating when eating appears as an available continuation. That availability depends on bodily capacity, sedimented history, interpretation, salience, social context, and current constraint.

Marian and Peter can face the same steak without inhabiting the same action field. For Peter, the steak remains dinner. For Marian, it becomes flesh and organized destruction. The physical object has not objectively lost edibility, and Marian has not merely acquired a new proposition about it. Her local action menu has changed.

The canned rice pudding provides an even cleaner comparison because the novel tracks the same kind of product before and during the crisis. Early in the novel, Marian taste-tests canned rice pudding at Seymour Surveys as a product for the time-saver market. Clara later serves the same convenient dessert at home. During Marian's refusals, the pudding temporarily becomes safe precisely because it seems artificial and remote from recognizable life. In Chapter 23, that protection fails when its globules become little cocoons, apparently full of latent life.

The pudding has not moved through four objective ontological categories. Its physical category remains comparatively stable while its situation changes: work sample, domestic convenience, refuge from organic history, and finally life-filled threat. The sequence therefore isolates what an affordance account adds. Edibility is not fixed by the object's material classification alone. The product's relation to Marian's current organization determines whether eating is locally available.

The larger food sequence is likewise not an instantaneous conversion to a single stable category. It broadens unevenly:

| Stage | What the novel depicts | Organizational consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary artificial product | Marian taste-tests canned rice pudding at work; Clara later serves the same kind of time-saving dessert at home. | The product begins inside ordinary consumer and domestic routines, before it becomes either refuge or threat. |
| Meat | Steak becomes cow, flesh, blood, butchery, and violence; the aversion broadens to several meats while fish remains possible. | Production history and animal body displace nourishment. |
| Egg | Len's chick story precedes Marian's refusal of an egg. | Possible or developing life becomes sufficient to bind. |
| Artificial refuge | Jelly, sweets, and canned rice pudding remain possible because they initially seem artificial. | Apparent distance from organic origin temporarily preserves edibility. |
| Plant | A carrot appears as a living root that may have suffered when pulled. | Livingness expands beyond animals. |
| Social concealment | Marian eats shrimp and soup at Trevor's dinner but transfers the meat course to Duncan. | Category boundaries remain uneven while refusal reorganizes social action. |
| Processed food | Rice-pudding globules become cocoons and sponge cake becomes thousands of lungs. | Processing can no longer prevent latent-life organization. |
| Mould and cleaning | Marian hesitates to clean because the mould may have a claim to continued life. | The pattern expands from eating into disposal and cleaning. |
| Near-total refusal | Toast and orange juice become unavailable and Marian fears starvation. | The affordance field narrows toward failure of her own continuation. |

This chronology matters. The rabbit is a pre-engagement hunting scene, not Marian's first food refusal. Artificial food first provides ordinary convenience and then temporary refuge; its later failure is therefore a transformation in Marian's action field, not just another item added to a list. Mould is not a food she tries to eat; it reorganizes cleaning. The processed-food failures occur before the mould scene. Duncan, not Marian, eats pumpkin seeds in the cinema. The sequence is structured, but it must not be made tidier than the novel.

## Deviation Binding

Ordinarily, the histories of food items can be compressed out of action. The diner need not encounter every causal stage between organism, harvest, processing, preparation, and plate. In Marian's experience those compressed histories return as binding deviations. Steak cannot remain merely steak once the cow and butcher matter to what can happen next. The egg cannot remain breakfast once the chick is recruited. The carrot cannot remain produce once uprooting and possible suffering bind.

This does not mean the foods possess an intrinsic deviation status. Deviation binding is organization-relative. A feature binds when removing it would alter admissible continuation. For Marian, traces of body, life, or destruction increasingly determine whether eating remains possible.

Refusal reduces the immediate conflict created by the bound item. Not eating the steak avoids eating what now appears as slaughtered flesh. But each local solution narrows the broader field of nourishment and social participation. As more categories bind under the same relation, avoidance becomes less reorganizable. The sequence approaches starvation not because Marian has stopped caring about continuation, but because her available terms for continuation have become mutually incompatible.

## Situation and Experience Dominance

Individual encounters reorganize Marian on local terms. Peter's rabbit story, Len's chick story, the carrot, the processed desserts, the mould, the party presentation, and the cake each recruit new constraints and affordances. In this component-relative sense, particular situations become dominant: changed context reorganizes what happens next.

Across those changes, however, a previously active organization increasingly assimilates each new setting. Restaurant, breakfast table, office party, grocery store, dinner with Duncan's housemates, apartment, engagement party, and post-party morning all become places where livingness, destruction, availability, or consumption continues. The physical setting and food object change; the governing relation persists.

This is **experience dominance** in IER's descriptive sense. It names continuity of organization across contextual change. It is not a force exerted by “experience,” a mechanism added to the pipeline, or evidence that Marian's interpretation is true. It says that what is already happening increasingly organizes each changed context as another place for the same relation to continue.

# 6. Empathy Without Access

The rabbit, cow, chick, carrot, and mould scenes can be read as a progressive broadening of Marian's participation in reproduced vulnerability. The fate of another living organization becomes locally structured through relations she can undergo: fear, injury, destruction, uprooting, assimilation, disposal.

IER empathy does not provide access to another system's experience. Marian does not enter the rabbit, cow, chick, carrot, or mould. Her experience remains locally instantiated. What reorganizes is her own situation through a reproduced topology of vulnerability.

This distinction becomes especially important when the sequence reaches plants and mould. Marian's identification does not establish that everything biologically alive is a Unified Experiential Field. IER contains no license to infer experiential status from behaviour, appearance, biological classification, or empathic force. Biological life, represented vulnerability, established experience, and categorical standing are not interchangeable.

The literary sequence is powerful precisely because Marian's local organization does not respect those distinctions. “Living” becomes sufficient to recruit the entire ethical and behavioural pattern. The breadth of identification explains the refusal's phenomenology; it does not certify its ontology.

# 7. Ethical Constraint Without an Innocent Meal

Marian's later ethical explanation discovers something real about ordinary continuation: organisms live through consumption, transformation, displacement, and destruction. Food does not appear without causal histories. Cleaning is not pure restoration; it destroys some organizations to preserve others. A body cannot continue without altering an environment.

The mould scene is where this recognition leaves the menu. Marian and Ainsley have stopped cleaning for weeks. Dirty dishes have accumulated, food is spoiling, and the refrigerator smells. Marian has eaten nothing that day and takes a vitamin. She feels an ordinary impulse to clean, then stops because the mould may have as much right to life as she does.

The scene should not be compressed into “Marian refuses mould.” She is not considering it as food. The action at stake is domestic maintenance: washing dishes, clearing spoiled material, and restoring conditions in which she can continue to live. Yet the same organization that made eating appear as destruction now makes cleaning appear as killing or removing another living organization. The problem has generalized from what Marian may take into herself to what she may destroy in order to maintain herself.

This is also another instance of crisis inside ordinary surroundings. Nothing supernatural has entered the kitchen. The dishes need washing, food has rotted, and mould is growing—the sort of conditions ordinary cleaning resolves without elaborate interpretation. But for Marian, the action now binds against an ethical rule whose consequences have become total. A normal chore no longer affords cleaning.

But Marian's rule compresses several different questions into one:

1. Is this biologically living, or was it once alive?
2. Is it experiential?
3. If experiential, what kind of harm is at issue?
4. What moral standing follows?
5. Does standing require absolute preservation?
6. What should happen when preservation of one continuation requires transformation or destruction of another?

IER separates these questions. Experiential moral harm concerns damage to the globally integrated intrinsic-constraint organization of a UEF. It does not follow from the category *biologically alive* alone. Categorical standing, where it applies, blocks treating an experiencer as morally negligible; it does not provide a universal preservation rule or a complete decision procedure.

This is why Marian's ethical organization becomes uninhabitable. Once anything that was or might still be living is excluded, eating becomes nearly impossible. Once apparent life makes cleaning suspect, ordinary maintenance becomes morally contaminated. Her own continuation then enters the conflict as starvation. The crisis is not that Marian cares too much in some generic sense. It is that one undifferentiated preservation rule has come to govern actions whose biological and ethical structures differ.

The novel need not be read as mocking ethical concern or proving that concern irrational. The refusal reveals dependence that ordinary eating compresses away. Its failure lies not in noticing implication but in attempting to turn implication into purity.

> Ethical constraint can remain real when no continuation is innocent and no general rule removes the need to resolve particular conflicts.

Marian's later return to steak does not establish that the cow, the butcher, or the earlier disgust meant nothing. It shows that her universal non-consumption rule no longer governs action. Ethical seriousness may persist after an impossible decision procedure releases.

# 8. Duncan and the False Exit

Duncan matters because he seems to offer a route outside the normal future organized around Peter. He is physically, socially, and rhetorically unlike Peter. He does not ask Marian to perform the same romantic script, and his low expectations can feel like relief. But the difference in style does not remove the user/used relation.

The novel makes that relation explicit. In Chapter 16 Duncan tells Marian that their intimacy means nothing to him and calls her another substitute for the laundromat. Later Marian recognizes that he is using her as an emotional outlet and finds, to her own surprise, that she does not mind. With Peter, declarations of love demand an appropriate answer. With Duncan, open self-absorption releases her from the obligation to perform reciprocal affection and interest.

That relief is real. It should not be erased simply because Duncan is instrumental. A situation can reduce one kind of constraint while preserving another. Marian has more room not to answer, not to reassure, and not to become Duncan's conventional partner. At the same time, Duncan treats her as a useful surface for his own regulation. He talks through his feelings, expects her to bring things to iron, and proposes sex largely as an experiment for himself. He imagines Marian as competent, knowledgeable, and unlikely to demand further attachment.

Marian's own participation remains mixed. Before dinner at Duncan's apartment, she removes her engagement ring and hides it in her purse. After leaving, she retrieves and replaces it. The gesture interrupts or conceals the engagement locally, but it does not end its governance. The future wife and Duncan's temporary outlet coexist within the same unresolved trajectory.

The engagement-party flight tests the alternative more severely. Marian escapes Peter's camera and seeks Duncan at the laundromat. They obtain a hotel room and attempt sex, but the encounter does not reorganize the crisis into safety or release. The next morning Marian cannot consume even toast or orange juice. She fears starvation, asks Duncan to remain with her, and eventually asks him to accompany her back to Peter. Duncan refuses both intervention and a continuing refuge. Marian realizes that what she wants is safety, but changing partners has not made safety available.

This does not make Duncan identical to Peter. Peter supplies a conventional future and participates in Marian's fear of assimilation. Duncan refuses conventional attachment and states his self-interest more openly. Their material relations to Marian differ. What persists is a more abstract organization: Marian remains available as something another person can use for a continuation organized primarily on his terms.

IER describes the episode as a failed change of situation. New location, companion, sexual activity, and self-description do not guarantee a new governing organization. The hotel follows the party, but the engagement, the eating crisis, the need for safety, and the user/used relation continue through it. Duncan is not a rescuer, but neither is he merely an irrelevant detour. He demonstrates that escape from Peter is not yet the same as disengagement from the organization in which Marian is made available.

> Duncan offers relief from one performance while reproducing instrumental use. The relief is genuine; the exit is not.

# 9. Refusal in the Wrong Domain

Marian resists repeatedly before she directly ends the engagement. She cries, runs, hides, argues, delegates, loses appetite, conceals food, removes and replaces her ring, avoids being photographed, seeks Duncan, and flees the party. Yet the engagement continues to organize practical continuation. She prepares for it, worries about it, and remains situated as Peter's future wife.

This is where `IER-disengagement.md` clarifies the novel. Stopping an activity does not establish that its governing organization has released. Changing objects or environments does not establish a new situation. An earlier organization may continue through new affordance surfaces.

Marian's food refusal changes what she is doing, but it does not by itself release the engagement trajectory. Neither do ring removal, secrecy, sex, or flight with Duncan. Restaurant, office, grocery store, apartment, dinner, party, laundromat, hotel, and ravine remain organized by the future she has not directly refused and by the crisis that accompanies it. Activity and location change while the earlier organization continues to govern.

## The Behavioural-Substitution Hypothesis

This article proposes a specific literary bridge hypothesis:

> When refusal of being made consumable or usable is not yet stably available, refusal appears as an inability to consume.

Compressed: **being made consumable or usable → direct refusal remains unstable → consumption becomes the domain of refusal → Marian cannot consume.**

The hypothesis does not claim that Marian consciously encodes a message in her eating, that a hidden bodily agent selects a symbol, or that every eating disorder preserves this relation. It is restricted to the organization of this novel.

The proposed substitution preserves a topology while changing target and reversing position:

- original relation: Marian is made available for assimilation into an externally organized future and for instrumental use within other people's continuations;
- substituted relation: Marian encounters other beings and objects as available for assimilation by her;
- substituted refusal: she cannot occupy the consumer position;
- explicit counter-model: she offers an edible representation instead of the subject.

This differs from IER's stabilized concept of **behavioural transfer**. Behavioural transfer describes a prior governing organization continuing through a changed domain. The food refusal does exhibit transfer because consumer/consumed and user/used organization persists across meat, egg, plant, processed food, cleaning, intimacy, appearance, and photography. The bridge hypothesis adds a more specific claim: the relation is not only preserved across domains; Marian's position within it is reversed.

That reversal explains why the behaviour can be meaningful without being a selected signal. It also explains why the refusal becomes broader than the initial conflict requires. Once consumption itself has become the surface of resistance, any item recruitable as living, vulnerable, or assimilable can become unavailable. A displaced exit narrows toward a threat to Marian's own viability.

Behavioural substitution should therefore remain explicitly provisional. It is what the article contributes for later theoretical evaluation, not a canonical mechanism the novel merely illustrates.

# 10. The Camera's Image

The engagement party makes the future role materially visible. Peter suggests a new dress and elaborate hair. Salon work and Ainsley's additions leave Marian artificial and difficult for herself to recognize. She has not simply chosen an outfit; she has been organized into a public presentation of the fiancée.

Peter's camera intensifies the relation. An earlier posed photograph is interrupted. Later, when Peter points the camera at Marian, she screams and runs. Her experience joins the camera to the hunting imagery: Peter appears as a marksman, and the camera as a potentially lethal instrument.

The novel supplies the hunter, marksman, and weapon relation. The further claim that the camera threatens **capture of a socially manufactured identity** is an interpretation. It is nevertheless a strong one. The image would not merely record Marian; it would fix the version constructed for Peter's party from a position outside Marian's authorship.

Here ordinary literary representation must be distinguished from IER's technical vocabulary. The photograph is a possible external representation: an artifact that preserves selected visual organization. It is not an internal representational content or an ontological ingredient of experience. Marian's resistance concerns what the artifact would do within the social situation—how it would stabilize, circulate, and ratify the made-up party woman.

The camera and cake are therefore opposed not because one is representation and the other is reality. Both are material constructions capable of preserving organization. They differ in practical authorship and use.

Peter attempts to frame Marian in an image prepared for his future. Marian later constructs an image whose materials, form, features, and presentation she authors herself.

# 11. The Cake as a Material Model

After the failed detour through Duncan and the near-total food refusal, Marian returns home. Peter telephones about her disappearance from the party. Marian does not explain the night to him; she invites him to come for tea. She then buys new ingredients, bakes without a recipe, cuts the cake into the shape of a woman, and decorates it with a pink party dress, green eyes, and hair resembling her party presentation (Chapter 30).

The cake is not merely a symbol supplied for the reader to decode. It is an action within the situation. Marian builds it, presents it to Peter, accuses him of trying to destroy or assimilate her, and explicitly offers the cake as a substitute.

In IER, a model is an organization used to project bounded consequence. It need not be a stored inner picture or a complete copy. The cake functions as a model because it preserves enough of the disputed relation to make a continuation inspectable: **woman → made attractive and available → presented to a consumer → destroyed through assimilation.**

The model is materially selective. It preserves the party dress, hair, femininity, attractiveness, edibility, and offer. It does not preserve Marian's subjecthood. That omission is the point. The cake can be consumed without the experiential subject being assimilated into the future it models.

## A Model That Changes Its Situation

Models do not stand outside the situations in which they are used. Once the cake exists on the table, it reorganizes local salience, interpretation, and affordances. What had been distributed across engagement, food, dress, camera, hunting, and fear becomes available for direct action.

Before the cake, Marian can flee, hide, refuse food, transfer meat, seek Duncan, or run from the camera. These actions resist parts of the situation without stably refusing its governing continuation. With the cake present, Marian can accuse Peter, name the substitution, and offer an alternative object for consumption.

The cake does not magically cure Marian. It changes the action field by giving the disputed relation a manipulable external form. A direct refusal that had remained unstable becomes locally available.

Peter leaves without eating. The novel does not state his internal reason. The article must therefore not report that he understands the metaphor, recognizes his guilt, or cannot tolerate the truth. His departure is text; his interpretation is underdetermined.

Marian then becomes hungry and begins eating the cake at the feet. This can be read as consumption of a constructed substitute rather than assent to her own assimilation. But that reading remains an inference. Ainsley interprets the act as rejection of femininity; Marian replies that it is only a cake. The novel gives no single explanatory voice final authority.

# 12. Authorship Across the Trajectory

The cake is a turning point, but it should not be isolated as one sovereign decision. IER trajectory authorship locates authorship in the persistence of owned constraint redistribution across time. Marian's final action depends on what has already happened: the dissolution dream, rabbit, flight, engagement, delegated decisions, narration shift, food aversion, ethical interpretation, normalizing reassurance, concealment, mould, Duncan's instrumental use, temporary ring removal, makeover, camera, failed sexual exit, starvation fear, Peter's call, and the practical work of baking.

The cake condenses this history into action. Its construction follows a recognizable phenomenological sequence:

- Peter calls about Marian's disappearance, and she makes another meeting available by inviting him for tea;
- buying ingredients becomes available;
- baking, cutting, and decorating sustain an intention across time;
- presenting the cake turns the model into confrontation;
- Peter's departure changes the continuation;
- Marian's hunger and eating register another reorganization.

None of this requires a selector outside the pipeline. The action is authored because Marian bears and redistributes the relevant constraint across the trajectory. The cake is not proof that she was previously non-agentive. It is a new organization of agency that makes direct refusal more available than displaced refusal.

## Disengagement and Rebinding

Disengagement occurs when a previously governing behavioural organization ceases to organize continuation. Residue may remain. The history need not disappear, and every effect need not reverse.

Marian's earlier substitutions do not disengage her from the engagement. The confrontation does. After Peter leaves, continuation no longer has to preserve the upcoming marriage. Marian can eat the cake, describe herself in the first person again, clean the apartment, say that she has left Peter, and eat steak.

The close sequence supports disengagement and rebinding without proving total transformation. The engagement loses its governing role. Another organization becomes possible: Marian participates more directly in maintaining her own apartment, appetite, narration, and immediate future.

This is **reorganizability**, not purification. Reorganizability is the capacity for current organization to alter under changing constraint. Marian's world has not become unconstrained, and she has not recovered an original untouched self. The available continuations have changed enough that she is no longer forced to reproduce the same future through every domain.

The return of appetite is therefore neither a biological theorem nor a simple moral. It is one component of a larger reorganization whose other components include direct speech, broken engagement, first-person narration, cleaning, and altered action.

# 13. Consumer Without Innocence or a Single Cause

Chapter 31 returns to first person the next day. Marian cleans dirty dishes, the refrigerator, and rotten food. She reports eating steak. Duncan first wonders whether Peter had been trying to destroy Marian or Marian had been trying to destroy Peter. He then dismisses the difference by calling her a consumer. Marian gives him the remaining woman-cake, and he eats its face and hair.

The ending refuses three easy resolutions.

The first is ethical purity. Marian has not found a way to continue without consuming, transforming, or destroying. Cleaning removes some forms of life and decay to sustain others. Eating steak re-enters the causal history that the earlier refusal made salient. Duncan's eating of the cake restores ordinary consumption even to the object that had reorganized Marian's situation.

The second is complete liberation. The return of “I” does not prove that Marian has escaped culture, consumer society, gender, embodiment, dependency, or sedimented history. She remains organized within a world of other people, material needs, and inherited roles. She remains a consumer.

The third is a single causal verdict. Marian explicitly accuses Peter of trying to destroy or assimilate her, and the article takes that accusation seriously. The hunting, makeover, camera, and ready-made future support it. But Duncan's final reversal prevents Peter from becoming a total explanation whose motives and causal power are known from outside. Marian has also participated in, resisted, delegated, concealed, sought substitutes for, and finally terminated the trajectory. Duncan's alternative does not erase asymmetry or make Marian responsible for her own crisis. It preserves the novel's refusal to settle the direction of destruction as one uncontested fact.

But the final label is not merely a reversal that makes the entire crisis pointless. At the beginning of the central trajectory, Marian is becoming available for an externally authored assimilation and repeatedly accepting relief through other people's use of her. At the end, she can participate in consumption without accepting her own equivalence to the consumable model. She has not abolished the consumer/consumed or user/used relation; she has changed her position within it and recovered practical authorship over the immediate continuation.

This makes the ending ethically harder and structurally stronger than a purity narrative. Marian's own continuation matters. The inability to preserve every living organization does not make harm imaginary. The reality of implication does not require self-erasure.

> Marian does not become innocent of consumption. She becomes less available for consumption by a future she has not authored.

# 14. What *The Edible Woman* Contributes to IER

This article has treated the novel as an application, but the traffic is not entirely one-way. Fiction can preserve a complex organization across scenes, characters, objects, and narrative form more effectively than a single abstract example. *The Edible Woman* gives IER several unusually clear structures to examine.

## 14.1 Behaviour Can Reorganize Before Explanation

Marian's eating changes before her ethical theory stabilizes. The sequence illustrates why an interpretation should not automatically be treated as either the hidden cause or the privileged truth of the behaviour it explains. Later interpretation can organize future salience without having initiated the pattern.

## 14.2 Crisis Can Remain Real Without Social Recognition

Clara's wedding-nerves explanation and Peter's reassurance do not resolve Marian's condition. They show that a severe change in one subject's action field can remain invisible within ordinary shared descriptions. Public continuity and experiential viability are related but non-identical.

## 14.3 Relief Can Coexist With Instrumental Use

Marian's time with Duncan reduces the demand to perform conventional affection, yet Duncan openly uses her as a substitute, outlet, and experiment. The novel prevents the easy equation of relief with freedom or of unconventionality with non-instrumental relation.

## 14.4 Refusal Can Move Across Domains

Running, hiding, not eating, transferring meat, fleeing photography, baking, confronting, and eating are not one behaviour. Yet they can preserve related organization. The novel supports analysis at the level of governing relations rather than surface acts alone.

## 14.5 Transfer Can Include Positional Reversal

IER behavioural transfer already describes organization continuing through a changed domain. The novel suggests a narrower possibility worth later theoretical development: a relation may persist while the participant's position reverses. Being organized as consumable becomes refusal to consume. The bridge term **behavioural substitution** names that proposed pattern here.

## 14.6 A Material Model Can Reorganize the Situation It Models

The cake does not only express Marian's situation. Its construction and use change what can be done. It preserves enough structure to support consequence projection, accusation, substitution, and direct refusal. The case demonstrates why external models belong inside environmental coupling rather than outside action.

## 14.7 Disengagement Is Not Stopping

Marian changes activities and locations repeatedly without releasing the engagement's governance. She runs, eats differently, visits Duncan, and leaves the party, but the same future continues to organize what follows. Disengagement occurs only when continuation no longer has to reproduce that future.

## 14.8 Ethical Recognition Does Not Produce Closure

The novel makes biological implication difficult to ignore while showing the failure of universal non-consumption. IER can preserve both sides: harm and standing constrain action, but neither supplies a complete algorithm for innocent continuation.

# 15. Structural Summary

The article's complete reading can be compressed into five movements.

| Movement | Narrative organization | IER redescription |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A disturbance enters a normal future | Bodily dissolution, tears, flight, proposal, delegated decisions, and ordinary wedding preparation coexist before categorical food refusal. | A socially intelligible continuation can remain externally normal while becoming increasingly non-integrated and unlivable for Marian. |
| Food reorganizes before explanation | Meat, egg, rice pudding, carrot, processed food, and eventually cleaning cease to support ordinary continuation before Marian's ethical account fully stabilizes. | Autonomic foregrounding, interpretation, salience, affordance reorganization, and narrowing occur within one subject. |
| The false exit preserves use | Duncan relieves Marian of conventional romantic performance but treats her as a substitute and outlet; ring removal, sex, and flight do not restore eating or safety. | New activity and companion do not guarantee a new situation; relief can coexist with instrumental use without producing disengagement. |
| Refusal transfers and reverses position | Livingness, destruction, consumption, and availability persist across food, mould, intimacy, appearance, and photography. | Experience-dominant organization and behavioural transfer preserve the relation; behavioural substitution names the proposed reversal from being consumable to being unable to consume. |
| Marian builds a counter-model | The woman-cake is authored, presented as a substitute, eaten, and followed by the return of first person and appetite. | A material model reorganizes the action field, supports direct refusal and disengagement, and increases reorganizability without producing purity or causal certainty. |

The novel's trajectory can therefore be stated without a hidden knower, a metaphysical true self, or a magical decision:

> Marian undergoes a severe but socially unrecognized reorganization inside a future everyone around her can continue to call normal. She repeatedly resists becoming consumable or usable before she can refuse that organization directly. The relation reorganizes eating and then cleaning until ordinary continuation becomes unavailable; Duncan changes its style without providing an exit. By constructing an edible model of the party woman and offering it as a substitute, Marian makes the relation actionable outside herself. Direct refusal becomes available, the engagement ceases to govern continuation, and appetite and first-person narration return. She does not escape consumption or receive a final causal explanation. She becomes less available as the assigned object of someone else's continuation.

# 16. Non-Claims and Drafting Boundaries

This article does not claim that:

- the Chapter 6 dissolution dream supplies a diagnosis, prophecy, or complete cause;
- the rabbit is Marian's first food refusal;
- the narration becomes third person at the moment she stops eating;
- Marian loses subjecthood during Part Two;
- Marian's body is a separate agent or source of privileged knowledge;
- Peter alone causes the crisis;
- Clara's and Peter's normalizing responses show that Marian is not in serious crisis;
- Marian lacks all conscious resistance before the cake;
- Peter successfully photographs the party image;
- Peter's reason for leaving is known;
- Duncan rescues Marian;
- Duncan supplies a non-instrumental alternative simply because he rejects conventional romance;
- Marian's relief at not minding Duncan's use means that the use is absent or uncomplicated;
- mould is a food Marian refuses;
- every living organism in the novel is a UEF;
- all biological consumption is experiential moral harm;
- the cake restores a true self or proves full recovery;
- Duncan's final reversal proves that Marian caused Peter to destroy her or erases the asymmetries documented earlier;
- every secondary plot or variable in Marian's life must be mapped for this focused argument;
- behavioural substitution is already canonical IER theory.

These exclusions are not incidental cautions. They preserve the difference between text, interpretation, and theory on which the article depends.
