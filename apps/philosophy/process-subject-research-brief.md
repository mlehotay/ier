# Process-Subject Research Brief

## Status

Status: draft
Authority: non-canonical
Publication: public research brief

## Research question and literature needs

This brief defines a self-directed literature search in philosophy of mind. It
develops a process account of identity theory in the tradition of U. T. Place’s
“Is Consciousness a Brain Process?” and J. J. C. Smart’s “Sensations and Brain
Processes.” Place and Smart identify conscious experiences with physical brain
processes. The project asks whether this identity claim can be extended from
conscious states to the subject of experience itself.

More specifically, can the subject of experience—the entity that is
conscious—be identified with a particular physical process continuing through
time? On such a view, the subject would not be an organism, brain, self, or
other entity that *has* conscious experiences; rather, the continuing physical
process itself would be the conscious subject.

There is a substantial literature on dynamical theories of consciousness and
on consciousness as an ongoing neural process. This question is narrower. The
relevant work would defend, consider, or reject a strict identity claim about
the **subject itself**, rather than claim that neural dynamics merely
constitute, realize, generate, support, or provide the substrate for
consciousness.

Existing discussions address conscious subjects, selves, neural processes, and
diachronic identity, but it remains unclear whether this precise
process-identity claim has already been proposed, which terminology describes
it, and where the relevant debates are located in the philosophical literature.

Matthew Owen’s [“The Neglected Conscious Subject in Consciousness
Science”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.06.009) sharpens the question.
Owen argues that consciousness science often discusses conscious states
without adequately identifying their subject. He develops a nonphysicalist
answer; the present search concerns physicalist alternatives, especially
accounts on which the subject is identified with an ongoing physical process
rather than with something that bears or contains experiences.

The initial research task is to identify the relevant terminology, principal
authors and debates, appropriate databases, and effective search strategies. A
useful starting set would include an accessible overview of the relevant
literatures and a small selection of central peer-reviewed papers, especially
work at the intersection of mind-brain identity theory, process ontology,
dynamical theories of consciousness, and theories of the subject of experience.
