# Problems of Physicalism: A Research Inventory

## Status and scope

**Status:** draft, non-canonical research inventory
**Authority:** working document; no IER claim, definition, or revision is made here

This document collects major problems for physicalism in the philosophy of mind and adjacent metaphysics. It is deliberately diagnostic rather than defensive. It does not ask how Informational Experiential Realism (IER) answers any problem, and inclusion does not imply that the problem succeeds.

“Physicalism” names a family of theses rather than one theory. A problem may target:

- physicalism in general;
- reductive or identity physicalism;
- nonreductive physicalism;
- functionalist, supervenience, realization, or grounding formulations;
- a priori rather than a posteriori physicalism; or
- global physicalism rather than physicalism about the mind alone.

The target must therefore be recorded alongside the objection. Apparent solutions sometimes work by changing the version of physicalism under discussion.

## Orientation: different kinds of problem

The literature does not consistently use titles of the form “the X problem for physicalism.” Some challenges are called problems, others gaps, arguments, dilemmas, or objections. They also ask importantly different questions:

| Kind | Governing question | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formulation | What does physicalism assert? | Hempel’s dilemma; the completeness and sufficiency problems |
| Metaphysical | Could all facts really be physical facts? | Zombies; knowledge argument; modal argument |
| Explanatory | Would complete physical knowledge explain experience? | Explanatory gap; hard problem; structure-and-dynamics argument |
| Causal | Can mentality make a difference in a physically complete world? | Mental causation; causal exclusion; epiphenomenalism |
| Realization | How do higher-level mental kinds relate to physical realizers? | Multiple realizability; realization regress; causal inheritance |
| Phenomenal organization | What physically constitutes one experience or subject? | Unity/binding; boundary; grain and temporal individuation |
| Semantic and normative | How can physical facts fix content or correctness? | Intentionality; rule-following; mental-content causation |

These families overlap, but they should not be collapsed. In particular, the structure-and-dynamics argument concerns what consciousness **is and how it is explained**; causal exclusion concerns what irreducible mental properties **do**.

## I. Defining physicalism

### 1. The condition problem: what is “physical”?

**Question:** Which properties, entities, relations, or facts count as physical?

The slogan “everything is physical” is not informative until “physical” is specified. Theory-based definitions appeal to what physics describes; object-based definitions appeal to paradigmatic physical objects and their constituents. Both invite disputes about circularity, fundamentality, dispositional versus categorical properties, and whether mentality is excluded by definition.

**Primary target:** every formulation of physicalism, prior to assessing its truth.

**Representative literature:** Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §§4.1–4.7; Stoljar, *Physicalism* (2010); Montero and Papineau, “A Defence of the Via Negativa Argument for Physicalism” (2005).

### 2. Hempel’s dilemma

**Question:** Is physicalism tied to current physics or ideal future physics?

If “physical” means what current physics posits, physicalism is probably false because present physics is incomplete. If it means what an ideal future physics will posit, the thesis threatens to become indeterminate or trivial: future physics might include properties that current physicalists classify as mental. The dilemma challenges the content of physicalism, not directly the existence of conscious experience.

**Primary target:** theory-based definitions of the physical.

**Representative literature:** Hempel, “Reduction: Ontological and Linguistic Facets” (1969); Crane and Mellor, “There Is No Question of Physicalism” (1990); Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §4.3.

### 3. The panpsychism problem

**Question:** Can a definition of “physical” exclude fundamental mentality without begging the question?

If fundamental properties may be both physical and experiential, then some panpsychist worlds satisfy familiar supervenience formulations of physicalism. But defining the physical simply as the non-mental threatens to rule out mind–brain identities by stipulation: if pain is identical with a physical property, that property is both mental and physical. The resulting problem is to formulate physicalism so that it is substantively distinct from panpsychism while remaining hospitable to psychophysical identity.

This is the literature’s closest established counterpart to the thought that physicalism needs “rescue from panpsychism.” It is partly a problem about the identity of the physicalist position, and partly a question about whether realistic physicalism is driven toward panpsychism.

**Primary target:** object-based and via-negativa definitions; supervenience physicalism; realistic or Russellian forms of physicalism.

**Representative literature:** Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §§4.4–4.5; Montero and Papineau (2005); Wilson, “On Characterizing the Physical” (2006); Strawson, “Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism” (2006).

### 4. The completeness or dependence problem

**Question:** What relation must everything bear to the physical—identity, realization, grounding, constitution, necessitation, or supervenience?

Token identity can be too weak because physical particulars might instantiate irreducibly nonphysical properties. Type identity can be too strong because higher-level kinds may be multiply realizable. Realization and grounding avoid some difficulties but require substantive accounts of what those relations are and why they secure physicalism.

**Primary target:** physicalism as a general metaphysical thesis.

**Representative literature:** Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §2; Melnyk, *A Physicalist Manifesto* (2003); Wilson, “Metaphysical Emergence: Weak and Strong” (2015); Bennett, *Making Things Up* (2017).

### 5. The sufficiency problem for supervenience

**Question:** Does metaphysical necessitation by the physical amount to physicalism?

Supervenience says that no mental difference is possible without a physical difference. Critics argue that this is not sufficient for physicalism. Necessitation dualism can posit mental properties that are distinct from physical properties yet necessarily correlated with them. If coherent, such a view satisfies supervenience while denying physicalism.

**Primary target:** supervenience and necessity formulations.

**Representative literature:** Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §2.1; Fine, “Essence and Modality” (1994).

## II. Consciousness and qualitative character

### 6. The explanatory gap

**Question:** Why should a given physical state feel exactly as it does?

Even if pain is correlated with, realized by, or identical to a neural state, a complete physical account appears to leave open why that state has a painful quality rather than another quality or none. Levine introduced the term for an apparent failure of intelligible explanation. The gap may be epistemic rather than ontological, so by itself it need not refute physicalism.

**Primary target:** reductive explanation and psychophysical identity claims; especially a priori physicalism.

**Representative literature:** Levine, “Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap” (1983); Levine, *Purple Haze* (2001).

### 7. The hard problem of consciousness

**Question:** Why and how does physical processing involve subjective experience at all?

Functional and mechanistic explanations can address discrimination, access, report, attention, memory, and behavioral control. The hard problem asks why performance of these functions is accompanied or constituted by experience. It is broader than any one thought experiment and more explicitly explanatory than the knowledge or zombie arguments.

**Primary target:** reductive and functional physicalist explanations of phenomenal consciousness.

**Representative literature:** Chalmers, “Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness” (1995); Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996).

### 8. The knowledge argument

**Question:** Would complete physical knowledge include all knowledge about experience?

Mary knows all physical facts about color vision while confined to a black-and-white environment. When she first sees red, she appears to learn what red looks like. If she acquires a new fact, then the physical facts were incomplete. Physicalist replies dispute whether she gains a fact, a recognitional ability, acquaintance, or a new mode of presentation.

**Primary target:** claims that all facts or propositional knowledge are physically derivable; a priori physicalism in particular.

**Representative literature:** Jackson, “Epiphenomenal Qualia” (1982) and “What Mary Didn’t Know” (1986); Lewis, “What Experience Teaches” (1988); Nida-Rümelin and O Conaill, “Qualia: The Knowledge Argument.”

### 9. The conceivability or zombie argument

**Question:** Is a physical duplicate without consciousness conceivable and therefore possible?

A philosophical zombie duplicates all physical structure, function, behavior, and history while lacking phenomenal consciousness. If such a world is metaphysically possible, physical facts do not necessitate phenomenal facts and physicalism is false. Controversy centers on whether zombies are ideally conceivable, whether conceivability indicates possibility, and whether psychophysical identities can be necessary a posteriori.

**Primary target:** supervenience physicalism and other formulations that require physical duplicates to be duplicates simpliciter.

**Representative literature:** Chalmers, *The Conscious Mind* (1996); Chalmers, “Consciousness and Its Place in Nature” (2002); “Zombies,” *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

### 10. The modal argument against mind–brain identity

**Question:** If identities are necessary, how can pain and its proposed physical basis appear separable?

Kripke argues that genuine identities involving rigid designators are necessary. Yet it seems possible for pain to occur without C-fiber firing, or C-fiber firing without pain. Unlike ordinary scientific identities such as heat and molecular motion, the appearance of contingency cannot easily be explained by a gap between the phenomenon and how it appears, because pain’s phenomenal character is central to what pain is.

**Primary target:** type-identity theories and some a posteriori physicalisms.

**Representative literature:** Kripke, *Naming and Necessity* (1980), Lecture III; Hill, *Sensation and a New Theory of Mind* (1991); Papineau, *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002).

### 11. The structure-and-dynamics argument

**Question:** Can truths framed wholly in structural, relational, causal, and dynamical terms explain intrinsic phenomenal character?

On a common reading of physics, physical descriptions specify structures, dispositions, equations, and patterns of causal evolution. Purely structural-dynamical premises appear to entail only further structural-dynamical truths. Phenomenal truths—what red looks like or pain feels like—do not appear exhausted by truths of that form. The argument is often used to motivate Russellian monism, which supplements physics’s structural description with claims about the intrinsic nature of physical properties.

**Primary target:** reductive materialism and physicalisms that restrict physical truth to structure and dynamics.

**Representative literature:** Chalmers, “Consciousness and Its Place in Nature” (2002); Alter, “The Structure and Dynamics Argument against Materialism” (2016).

### 12. The subjectivity or first-person problem

**Question:** Can objective physical descriptions entail perspectival facts—facts about what the world is like for a subject?

Physical science characteristically describes systems from an intersubjective or third-person standpoint. Conscious experience is essentially presented from a point of view. The challenge is not merely that introspective and scientific concepts differ, but that an objective account may omit the perspectival form of phenomenal facts.

**Primary target:** reductive physicalism about consciousness and claims of explanatory completeness.

**Representative literature:** Nagel, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” (1974); Levine (2001); Howell, *Consciousness and the Limits of Objectivity* (2013).

### 13. The phenomenal-concept or materialist-constraint problem

**Question:** Can a physicalist explain, in wholly materialistically acceptable terms, why phenomenal and physical concepts seem explanatorily disconnected?

The phenomenal concept strategy locates the apparent gap in our concepts rather than in reality. Its burden is to explain the distinctive epistemic features of phenomenal concepts—directness, privacy, recognitional deployment, and apparent revelation—without presupposing irreducible phenomenal properties. Levine calls the demand that this explanation itself meet physicalist standards the “materialist constraint.”

**Primary target:** a posteriori physicalism and phenomenal-concept defenses of it.

**Representative literature:** Stoljar, “Physicalism and Phenomenal Concepts” (2005); Chalmers, “Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap” (2007); Levine, “Phenomenal Concepts and the Materialist Constraint” (2007).

### 14. Inverted and absent qualia

**Question:** Could physical or functional organization remain fixed while qualitative character changes or disappears?

Inverted-spectrum cases imagine two subjects with the same functional organization but systematically different color experiences. Absent-qualia cases imagine the same organization without experience. If either is possible, functional or organizational facts do not suffice for phenomenal facts.

**Primary target:** functionalism and organizationally invariant physicalist theories; broader physicalism only when the cases are made physically duplicate.

**Representative literature:** Block and Fodor, “What Psychological States Are Not” (1972); Block, “Troubles with Functionalism” (1978); Shoemaker, “The Inverted Spectrum” (1982).

## III. Causation, emergence, and higher levels

### 15. The problem of mental causation

**Question:** How can beliefs, desires, intentions, and experiences cause bodily behavior in a physically closed world?

Physicalists generally accept that mental events are causally relevant and that physical effects have sufficient physical causes. The task is to explain mental causation without causal gaps, systematic overdetermination, or the disappearance of mental properties from the causal story.

**Primary target:** any physicalism that recognizes mental explanation, with different pressures on different versions.

**Representative literature:** Davidson, “Mental Events” (1970); Heil and Mele, eds., *Mental Causation* (1993); Robb and Heil, “Mental Causation.”

### 16. The causal exclusion problem

**Question:** If a physical effect already has a sufficient physical cause, what causal work remains for a distinct mental cause?

The standard inconsistent-looking package comprises mental causation, physical causal completeness, irreducibility of mental properties, and exclusion of distinct sufficient causes except in genuine overdetermination. If the neural cause is sufficient and the mental property is not identical with it, the mental property appears excluded. Reduction may avoid competition, but at the cost of the nonreductive thesis.

**Primary target:** nonreductive physicalism, emergentism, and some realization theories—not reductive identity physicalism as such.

**Representative literature:** Kim, “The Myth of Nonreductive Materialism” (1989); Kim, *Mind in a Physical World* (1998); Bennett, “Mental Causation” (2008); Kallestrup, “The Causal Exclusion Argument” (2006).

### 17. The epiphenomenalism problem

**Question:** If phenomenal character contributes no physical causal power, why do reports and beliefs track it?

A view on which experience is a causally inert accompaniment to physical processing must explain the apparent systematic connection between experiences and our judgments, reports, memories, and evolutionary history concerning them. The problem is especially sharp when all behavior has an independently sufficient physical explanation.

**Primary target:** property dualism and nonreductive views vulnerable to exclusion; it also pressures physicalist accounts that identify consciousness only by correlation and give it no explanatory role.

**Representative literature:** Jackson (1982); Chalmers (1996), ch. 5; Robinson, “Epiphenomenalism.”

### 18. The problem of strong or radical emergence

**Question:** How could wholly non-experiential physical conditions produce a novel experiential reality?

Weak emergence concerns higher-level patterns that are surprising or difficult to derive but remain grounded in lower-level organization. Strong emergence adds genuinely novel properties or powers not reducible to, or deducible from, the base. Applied to consciousness, critics ask how experience could arise from constituents whose nature contains no experiential feature, and whether the proposed emergence amounts to an unexplained brute law.

**Primary target:** emergent physicalism and views that distinguish experience from its physical basis while retaining physicalist credentials.

**Representative literature:** Broad, *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925); Kim, “Making Sense of Emergence” (1999); Strawson (2006); O’Connor and Wong, “Emergent Properties.”

### 19. The realization and causal-inheritance problem

**Question:** Why do physically realized higher-level mental properties possess their advertised causal powers?

Functional realization defines a mental property by a causal role and identifies a physical property as its occupant. Difficulties arise over whether the role property is distinct from the occupant, whether its powers are inherited from the realizer, whether it becomes causally redundant, and whether defining role properties requires further properties in an explanatory regress.

**Primary target:** functionalist and realization physicalism.

**Representative literature:** Melnyk (2003); Shoemaker, *Physical Realization* (2007); Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §2.3.

### 20. Multiple realizability

**Question:** Can a single mental kind be identical with one physical kind if it can be realized in many physical ways?

Pain may occur in humans, other animals, aliens, or artificial systems with very different physical implementations. If so, pain cannot simply be identical with one narrow neurophysiological type. The argument is classically aimed at type identity and reduction, though it does not by itself refute token physicalism or realization-based physicalism.

**Primary target:** type-identity theory and some forms of intertheoretic reduction.

**Representative literature:** Putnam, “Psychological Predicates” (1967); Fodor, “Special Sciences” (1974); Bickle, “Multiple Realizability.”

## IV. Unity, subjects, and individuation

### 21. The unity or binding problem

**Question:** What makes distributed physical activity one unified conscious field rather than a collection of separate experiences?

Neural processing is spatially and functionally distributed, while experience normally presents as a single differentiated whole. Feature binding asks how color, shape, location, sound, and other contents belong to one perceptual scene. Subject unity asks why these contents are experiences of one subject. These questions are related but not identical.

**Primary target:** physicalist theories of consciousness generally, especially decompositional or modular accounts.

**Representative literature:** Bayne and Chalmers, “What Is the Unity of Consciousness?” (2003); Bayne, *The Unity of Consciousness* (2010); “The Unity of Consciousness,” *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*.

### 22. The boundary or subject-individuation problem

**Question:** Which physical parts and processes constitute one conscious subject, and why does the subject stop there?

Physical systems are causally open, nested, overlapping, and describable at many scales. A theory must distinguish constitutive from merely causal involvement and say why a brain, subsystem, organism, group, or larger environment is the bearer of a particular experience. The challenge includes split, overlapping, nested, and partially integrated candidate systems.

**Primary target:** system-level physicalist theories of consciousness; panpsychism faces a related but different combination or boundary problem.

**Representative literature:** Rosenberg, *A Place for Consciousness* (2004); Bayne (2010); Gómez-Emilsson and Percy, “The Boundary Problem for Phenomenal Consciousness” (2023).

### 23. The grain, level, or slicing problem

**Question:** At what spatial, temporal, and organizational scale is the conscious physical process individuated?

The same physical history can be partitioned into micro-events, neural assemblies, recurrent loops, organism-level processes, and longer temporal windows. If a theory associates consciousness with organization at one grain, it must explain why nearby alternative decompositions are not equally valid subjects or experiences. This is less canonically named than the hard problem or exclusion problem, but it is a recurring individuation burden.

**Primary target:** process, computational, dynamical, and information-theoretic physicalisms.

**Representative literature:** Wimsatt, “Reductionism and Its Heuristics” (2007); Hoel, Albantakis, and Tononi, “Quantifying Causal Emergence Shows That Macro Can Beat Micro” (2013); List, “Levels: Descriptive, Explanatory, and Ontological” (2019).

### 24. The diachronic unity and persistence problem

**Question:** What makes experiences at different times phases of one continuing subject?

Physical constituents, neural activity, functional organization, and system boundaries change. A physicalist account of the subject must specify the relation—causal, functional, biological, psychological, or processual—that preserves subject identity through ordinary change and explain what happens under fission, fusion, interruption, duplication, and reconstruction.

**Primary target:** physicalist accounts of the subject and personal identity; not an argument against minimal physicalism by itself.

**Representative literature:** Parfit, *Reasons and Persons* (1984), Part III; Dainton, *Stream of Consciousness* (2000); Schechtman, *Staying Alive* (2014).

## V. Intentionality, content, and normativity

### 25. The problem of intentionality or aboutness

**Question:** How can a physical state be about something, including something absent, fictional, or nonexistent?

Causal and informational relations can ground covariation, but representation also involves correctness and misrepresentation. A state can mean that *p* even when *p* is false. The physicalist owes an account of how determinate content and error conditions arise from physical organization and history.

**Primary target:** physicalism about representational content; causal, informational, and dispositional naturalization programs.

**Representative literature:** Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874/1995); Fodor, *A Theory of Content and Other Essays* (1990); Dretske, *Naturalizing the Mind* (1995); “Physicalism,” §5.2.

### 26. The normativity and rule-following problem

**Question:** How can descriptive physical dispositions determine what a thinker ought to infer or what a symbol means?

Actual dispositions include mistakes and are finite, whereas meaning appears to determine standards for indefinitely many correct and incorrect applications. The rule-following challenge asks what physical fact fixes one norm rather than another. It extends beyond linguistic meaning to rational inference and reasons for action.

**Primary target:** reductive dispositional accounts of meaning, content, and rational normativity.

**Representative literature:** Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982); Boghossian, “The Rule-Following Considerations” (1989); Gibbard, *Meaning and Normativity* (2012).

### 27. The causal relevance of broad content

**Question:** How can externally individuated content cause behavior through local physical mechanisms?

On semantic externalism, what a thought means partly depends on causal, social, or historical relations beyond the thinker. Yet bodily behavior is produced by local intrinsic mechanisms. Twin cases can therefore share intrinsic neural organization while differing in content. The challenge is to show how broad content, rather than only its local vehicle, makes a causal difference.

**Primary target:** physicalism combining content externalism with causally efficacious mental explanation.

**Representative literature:** Burge, “Individualism and the Mental” (1979); Fodor, “Making Mind Matter More” (1989); “Mental Causation,” §7.

### 28. The cognitive-phenomenology or “new hard problem”

**Question:** Does conscious thought have a proprietary phenomenal character, and if so, how is determinate meaning present in experience?

If consciously entertaining one proposition feels different from entertaining another even when sensory imagery is held fixed, physicalism must explain not only sensory qualia but conscious meaning. Goff calls the challenge of explaining how rich semantic content appears in phenomenal character a “new hard problem.”

**Primary target:** physicalist theories of phenomenal consciousness and intentional content.

**Representative literature:** Bayne and Montague, eds., *Cognitive Phenomenology* (2011); Pitt, “The Phenomenology of Cognition” (2004); Goff, “Does Mary Know I Experience Plus Rather Than Quus? A New Hard Problem” (2012).

## VI. Wider scope and methodology

### 29. The problem of abstracta

**Question:** If numbers, propositions, properties, or relations exist outside space and time, in what sense are they physical or physically grounded?

This is a problem for global physicalism rather than specifically physicalism about the mind. Mathematical and propositional entities appear neither identical with physical particulars nor straightforwardly realized by them. Nominalism can avoid the ontological commitment but carries its own explanatory burdens.

**Primary target:** global physicalism that quantifies over everything whatsoever.

**Representative literature:** Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §5.3; Field, *Science Without Numbers* (1980); Rabin, “Fundamentality Physicalism” (2022).

### 30. The methodological or explanatory-overreach problem

**Question:** What evidence supports a universal metaphysical thesis extending beyond established physical explanations?

The success of physical science in explaining physical phenomena supports causal completeness and methodological naturalism. It does not automatically establish that every actual fact is physical, that every special-science explanation reduces to physics, or that future inquiry cannot revise the physicalist ontology. Critics charge that physicalism can exceed its empirical warrant.

**Primary target:** arguments from scientific success to global or metaphysically necessary physicalism.

**Representative literature:** Crane and Mellor (1990); Chomsky, “Language and Nature” (1995); Stoljar, “Physicalism,” §5.4.

## Priority map for later IER assessment

This inventory is intentionally broader than IER’s present anti-panpsychist claim. A later assessment should not treat all entries as independent hurdles or as equally urgent.

| Priority | Problems | Why they belong together |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Central phenomenal challenge | 6–14 | Whether physical facts constitute, necessitate, or explain phenomenal facts |
| Causal and emergent challenge | 15–19 | Whether irreducible mentality can arise and remain causally efficacious under physical completeness |
| Subject-constitution challenge | 21–24 | What makes one experience, one bearer, and one continuing subject |
| Semantic challenge | 25–28 | How physical organization fixes aboutness, correctness, and conscious meaning |
| Definition of the position | 1–5 | Whether “physicalism” and its dependence relations are clear and nontrivial |
| Global-scope challenge | 29–30 | Whether physicalism extends beyond mind and beyond the evidence of current science |

The numbering is classificatory, not a claim that there are exactly thirty independent objections. Several entries are argumentative forms of a common underlying concern. For example, the knowledge argument, zombie argument, and structure-and-dynamics argument can all be used to support the claim that physical truth does not entail phenomenal truth, but they reach that conclusion through epistemic, modal, and explanatory routes respectively.

## Working cautions

- A challenge to reduction is not automatically a refutation of physicalism. Nonreductive, realization, grounding, and a posteriori versions may remain available.
- A persistent explanatory gap is not automatically an ontological gap. An argument is required to move from failure of explanation or deduction to metaphysical distinctness.
- A response that changes the subject—from phenomenal consciousness to access, report, cognition, or behavior—does not by itself answer the phenomenal problem.
- A theory that identifies experience with a physical process must still state the identity conditions of the process and explain why rival physical partitions are not equally eligible.
- Panpsychism does not simply eliminate the hard problem; it relocates explanatory burdens to combination, subject individuation, structural organization, and the relation between fundamental and macroexperience.
- “Physical determination” can mean supervenience, causal determination, realization, grounding, constitution, or identity. These are not interchangeable.
