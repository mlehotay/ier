# Process-Subject Literature Research Report

## Status

Status: complete, bounded literature finding  
Date: 6 September 2026  
Authority: non-canonical research report

## Executive finding

**Outcome A — explicit precedent.** Galen Strawson's 2003 account of the
“thin” subject is an explicit physical process-subject theory. Under a
materialist hypothesis, he identifies an experience, its subject, and its
content with the same short-lived, spatiotemporally bounded synergy of physical
“process-stuff.” He emphasizes that the relation is identity rather than mere
constitution. This is the clearest located precedent for the target claim.

The precedent is exact at the level of ontology but not at the level of
persistence. Strawson's thin subject is episode-scale: a change of content or
even a change in the ultimate parts of the synergy yields a numerically new
subject. A completely experienceless interval certainly ends it. The proposed
IER account instead treats one continuously instantiated physical regime as the
same experiential subject through changes in content, organization, and
participants, while agreeing that genuine cessation ends that subject. The
secure distinctive question is therefore not whether a physical process can be
called the subject. It is whether a process-subject can have principled,
non-mereological persistence conditions that permit change without admitting
gaps.

This is a bounded precedent finding, not a claim that Strawson exhausts the
literature or that no one else has combined these ideas. The searches also
located: (i) state-identity theories that do not identify the continuing
subject; (ii) capacity, substance, organism, experiential-process, and
self-process accounts; (iii) process-ontology resources for individuation and
persistence; and (iv) direct objections concerning bearerhood, overlap, unity,
and interrupted consciousness.

## Neutral statement of the thesis

This investigation asks whether the concrete subject of experience—the entity
that is conscious—can be numerically identical with one temporally continuing
physical process, rather than being an organism, brain, self, substance, or
other entity that has, contains, constitutes, realizes, supports, generates, or
represents experience.

The screening question was:

> Does this source address whether the concrete entity that is conscious is
> numerically identical with a temporally continuing physical process?

For this investigation, *subject of experience* means the concrete individual
whose experience it is. This definition does not assume that the subject is a
self, person, organism, substance, bundle, stream, or process. It also does not
assume that the subject outlasts consciousness.

## Scope and method

The search separated four questions that are often run together:

1. **State identity:** what an experience is.
2. **Subject ontology:** what the experiencer is.
3. **Synchronic unity:** what makes there be one subject at a time.
4. **Diachronic identity:** what makes a subject persist through change.

Sources were screened using fixed relation labels: numerical identity,
constitution, realization, grounding, causal production, causal dependence,
correlation, representation, parthood, analogy, or unclear. Primary texts and
author manuscripts were preferred. Publisher pages and scholarly indexes were
used to verify metadata. Search snippets served only as discovery leads.

Five tracks were run: exact identity; subject/bearer ontology; process
individuation and persistence; nearby physicalist or naturalist positions; and
objections/stress cases. The detailed queries and stopping reasons appear in
the separate search log. Core source records and access qualifications appear
in the source ledger.

## What the literature actually claims

### 1. The seed identity theories identify states, not the subject

U. T. Place argues that consciousness is a process in the brain and explains
what would justify treating first-person and physiological observations as
observations of the same process rather than correlated processes. J. J. C.
Smart argues that sensations are brain processes. Their shared relation is
**numerical identity**, but their principal relata are conscious states or
sensations and brain processes. Neither paper supplies an account of what the
continuing subject is or when it remains numerically the same subject. Place
and Smart therefore establish the identity-theory method while remaining false
friends for the narrower subject-identity thesis. See [Place
(1956)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1956.tb00560.x) and [Smart
(1959)](https://doi.org/10.2307/2182164).

Matthew Owen makes this omission explicit: consciousness research can discuss
states, contents, or neural correlates while leaving the bearer of those states
unidentified. The short 2024 commentary was not fully accessible during this
session, so its positive view is not assigned from snippets. Its bearer problem
is independently restated in the open-access organoid paper. There Owen, Hight,
and Hudetz distinguish Smart's state identity from the question of what entity
is conscious, and oppose reducing creature consciousness to the activity of a
proper part. Their positive proposal is a hylomorphic organism-level bearer,
not a process-subject. See [Owen
(2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.06.009) and [Owen, Hight, and
Hudetz (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12152-024-09577-y).

### 2. Strawson supplies the exact precedent

Strawson distinguishes thick subjects (whole creatures), traditional inner
subjects, and thin subjects that exist only while experience occurs. His
materialist construction makes the thin subject a spatiotemporally bounded
synergy of physical process-stuff. He then proposes that the physical process
that is the experience is the very same process as the subject and content.
The primary text explicitly contrasts identity with a looser constitutive
relation. The source therefore warrants all elements required for Level A:

- candidate subject: the occurrent thin subject;
- physical relatum: a bounded, synergistic neural/physical process;
- relation: **numerical identity**;
- temporal extent: non-zero but short-lived, illustrated as approximately two
  seconds;
- persistence: no survival of an experienceless interval, and no survival of
  a change in the synergy's parts or the relevant content.

The strongest primary passage is Strawson's equation of the process-stuff that
is the experience, subject, and content in §6. Earlier sections characterize
the subject as the set of lights *in the process of flashing*, not the set of
lights considered as a stored object. See [Strawson's author-accessible text,
especially §§2 and 6](https://consc.net/event/neh/papers/strawson1.htm) and the
[published article record](https://doi.org/10.1111/1533-6077.00015).

This finding should be stated with two qualifications. First, Strawson calls
the subject an object or substance as well as a processual entity; his
metaphysics treats physical objects generally as process-stuff. This does not
weaken the identity claim, but it means “process” is embedded in a
processualized substance ontology. Second, the theory is not a theory of a
long-lived subject that owns a sequence of changing experiences. It identifies
the subject with an occurrent episode of subjectivity.

### 3. Direct alternatives and objections

Henry Taylor gives the cleanest contrasting bearer ontology. He treats a
conscious experience as a property-exemplification event whose constituting
substance is the subject. The subject partially constitutes the event, but is
not identical with it and can exist without that particular experience.
Taylor explicitly presents Strawson's subject–experience identity as the most
prominent alternative and objects that its revisionary thin-subject concept is
not theoretically warranted. Taylor's relation is **constitution**, with an
asymmetric dependence of experience on subject. See [Taylor
(2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-019-01379-w).

Barry Dainton is the strongest near precedent. In “Selfhood and the Flow of
Experience,” he takes Strawson's identification of subjects-at-times with
brief episodes seriously, but argues that the continuity of ordinary
experience supports a subject whose essence is a persisting capacity for
unified experience. His later formulation says that subjects consist of
capacities for experience and that having an experience is the subject's
producing it. This is a direct theory of the subject, but the subject is a
capacity-system rather than the currently operating physical process, and the
relation to an experience is **causal production** rather than identity. See
[Dainton (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1163/9789401207904_009) and [Dainton
(2016)](https://doi.org/10.1093/arisup/akw007).

Helen Steward provides the most direct category objection found. While her
main target is the claim that organisms should be replaced by processes, she
argues that consciousness requires a subject and that it is difficult to see
how a set of processes could itself be that subject. Her positive view retains
an enduring animal/agent and treats the processes as activities of it. This is
an explicit rejection of replacing the bearer with processes, although her
wording targets a set of processes rather than Strawson's one bounded synergy.
See [Steward (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031819119000494).

William James is an important nonphysical antecedent. In his discussion of the
stream of thought, the passing thought performs the thinker's appropriative
work; this reduces the need for a separate ego. But James's psychological
proposal does not identify the subject with a physical process. Its relevance
is conceptual, not an exact materialist precedent. See [James, *Principles of
Psychology*, vol. 1](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/).

Evan Thompson likewise calls the self an experiential, enacted process rather
than a hidden entity. This is a genuine process account of selfhood, but the
self can come and go with modes of awareness, and the source does not equate
the concrete experiential subject with a particular physical process. It is a
nearby **analogy/unclear** relation, not Level A. See [Thompson's précis
(2016)](https://philpapers.org/rec/THOPOW).

### 4. Unity literature does not by itself settle subject ontology

Bayne and Chalmers distinguish subject unity (states had by one subject) from
phenomenal unity (states subsumed by one total phenomenal state). Subject unity
is definitionally thin and does not explain what the subject is. Their account
shows why inferring one subject directly from coordinated neural activity would
be invalid: a bridge is still required from physical integration to one total
phenomenal state and then to subject individuation. See [Bayne and Chalmers
(2003)](https://consc.net/papers/unity.html).

Recent work makes the counting problem sharper. Schwitzgebel and Nelson argue
that naturalistic views may permit fractional or indeterminate numbers of
subjects when the underlying processes overlap or integrate incompletely.
Roelofs develops the “too many minds” pressure: if a material object is a
subject, many overlapping material candidates may seem equally qualified.
Neither paper supplies the target identity, but both expose a burden for any
maximal-process boundary rule. See [Schwitzgebel and Nelson
(2026)](https://doi.org/10.1080/09515089.2025.2520364) and [Roelofs
(2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-024-02167-x).

## Bearer-ladder analysis

| Level | Claim | Literature status | Does it entail the target? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| B0 | An experience is of or for some subject | Strawson defends this; no-subject views dispute it | No |
| B1 | Experience has first-personal givenness or for-me-ness | Common in phenomenological and mineness debates | No; a structural feature is not yet an entity |
| B2 | Several contents form one total experience or belong to one subject | Bayne and Chalmers distinguish phenomenal and subject unity | No; unity can be stated while subject ontology remains open |
| B3 | Something instantiates, undergoes, or bears the experience | Taylor and Owen insist on a bearer | No; bearing normally contrasts with identity |
| B4 | One numerically identical subject persists through changing experiences | Dainton's capacity view and organism views permit this | No; the persisting subject may be a substance, organism, or capacity-system |
| B5 | The subject is a distinct or metaphysically simple bearer | Substance and hylomorphic accounts may support this | No; it rejects or bypasses process identity |

Strawson's distinctive move is to resist climbing from B0 to a distinct bearer:
occurrent subjectivity already is the thin subject. The cost is that B4 is
rejected for ordinary changes that thicker accounts treat as survival. A
continuing-process theory attempts a different combination: retain Strawson's
anti-distinct-bearer identity while recovering a constrained version of B4.

## Process individuation and persistence resources

The philosophy-of-biology process literature supplies useful machinery but
does not itself identify the conscious subject.

Daniel Nicholson argues that organisms are better conceived as dynamically
self-maintaining streams. Their persistence need not consist in material
identity; it can consist in a continuous causal trajectory that maintains
organization despite turnover. This is a Level C antecedent for allowing a
physical process to survive replacement of participants. See [Nicholson
(2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0007).

Johanna Seibt's general process theory offers several dimensions for process
individuation: spatiotemporal profile, participant structure, dynamic
composition, dynamic shape, and dynamic context. It also permits processes to
be more or less generic rather than forcing identity to track every ultimate
part. This is useful for articulating why one regime can continue while its
microphysical realization changes. See [Seibt
(2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0006).

James DiFrisco directly examines biological-process identity and persistence,
contrasting causal and spatiotemporal criteria. Thomas Pradeu revives
*genidentity*: following an individual through temporally ordered, causally
connected transformations. Together they provide vocabulary for continuous
process identity without static material sameness. See [DiFrisco
(2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0004) and [Pradeu
(2018)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0005).

These resources establish that turnover-compatible process persistence is a
respectable metaphysical option. They do **not** establish that the relevant
process is conscious, that its unity is phenomenal unity, or that it is the
subject rather than the subject's activity.

## Burdens on a strict continuing process-subject account

| Burden | Established resources | Remaining work |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individuation | Seibt supplies multiple dimensions; DiFrisco and Pradeu supply causal/spatiotemporal and genidentity criteria | Select and justify a non-arbitrary combination that excludes wider, narrower, and overlapping rivals |
| Unity | Bayne and Chalmers separate subject unity from phenomenal unity | Give a bridge principle from one physical process to one total phenomenal field without presupposing the subject |
| Persistence | Nicholson and Pradeu allow causal/organizational continuity through turnover | State which organizational changes preserve this particular subject and why those criteria are identity-fixing |
| Interruption | Strawson and Dainton explicitly expose the sleep problem | Decide whether a null interval terminates the subject; if yes, explain the consequences for ordinary self-reference and responsibility |
| Material turnover | Process biology makes turnover-compatible persistence coherent | Explain why participant replacement preserves the process while fission or loss of global organization does not |
| Fission and fusion | Split-brain, overlap, and mindmelding literature supplies stress cases | Give prospective, non-question-begging rules for one-to-two and two-to-one transitions |
| Identity rather than constitution | Place supplies the identity-theory explanatory model; Strawson supplies the direct precedent | Offer a positive identity argument; explanatory fit and physical covariance alone underdetermine identity |
| Circularity | Process ontologies can specify causal and dynamical profiles in nonexperiential terms | Demonstrate that the chosen profile does not mean “whatever physical process is the subject” |

The literature therefore divides the work unevenly. Process persistence and
turnover have substantial existing resources. Unity-to-subjecthood and the
positive identity argument remain only partially treated. The hardest burden
is boundary selection under overlap: a theory needs more than “maximal
integration,” because maximality relative to a chosen system boundary can
restate rather than solve the counting problem.

## Bounded finding: Outcome A

The classification is **Outcome A — explicit precedent**, supported as follows:

- **Strongest exact candidate:** Strawson's thin subject, numerically identical
  with a bounded synergy of physical process-stuff and with its experience.
- **Strongest near candidate:** Dainton's persisting capacity for unified
  experience. It is a subject theory with diachronic resources, but not an
  occurrent physical-process identity.
- **Most important false friend:** Place/Smart state identity. Their conscious
  state–brain process identities do not identify the continuing subject.
- **Best process-individuation framework:** Seibt's multidimensional process
  profile, supplemented by Nicholson's self-maintaining trajectory and
  Pradeu's genidentity.
- **Principal objection:** a process episode may be too short-lived and too
  dependent on its exact content to explain subject persistence (Taylor), and
  physical processes do not automatically qualify as bearers of experience
  (Steward).
- **Search limit:** only publicly reachable full text, abstracts, indexes, and
  citation chains were used. Owen 2024 and some books were not fully available.
  No universal novelty conclusion follows.

The exact precedent changes the defensible novelty claim. “The subject is a
physical process” is not new. A narrower contribution may remain: a strict
identity theory of a **temporally continuing** process-subject with explicit
criteria for unity, persistence through content and participant change, and
termination at genuine experiential gaps.

Two IER-facing conclusions follow.

First, the supplied specification clearly identifies **experience** with UEF
operation, while defining subjecthood as sustaining a UEF and saying there is
exactly one subject per UEF. Explanatory texts sometimes say that a UEF
“constitutes” one subject, yet elsewhere speak of “the persistence of a UEF as
an experiential subject.” Because constitution and identity are
non-interchangeable in the public debate, a later canonical review should make
the intended relation explicit in one formula. If the intended thesis is
`subject = continuing UEF operation`, “constitutes” invites precisely the
bearer gap the project is designed to close.

Second, IER's plausible distinctiveness lies in its identity conditions. Unlike
Strawson's strict micro-part/content criterion, IER permits content,
participation, and organization to change while one continuous regime remains.
Unlike Dainton's capacity view, it denies survival across a genuinely
experience-null interval. That middle position requires an argument grounded
in process genidentity and a non-arbitrary physical unity criterion.

## Limitations

- The search was broad but not exhaustive across every subscription database,
  language, dissertation, or unpublished manuscript.
- The full text of Owen's two-page 2024 commentary was inaccessible; only
  metadata and claims restated in the open 2025 article were used.
- Several book-length positions were checked through author précis, publisher
  abstracts, or accessible chapters rather than read in full.
- Citation chaining from Strawson largely returned discussions of
  subject–experience identity, panpsychism, phenomenal selves, and unity. It
  did not yield a second equally clear physical continuing-process account in
  two rounds, but this supports only a bounded search finding.
- “Process,” “event,” “activity,” “stream,” and “dynamic individual” are not
  uniform categories across authors. The report does not force them into one
  ontology.
- No claim here establishes that any actual system is conscious or that IER's
  proposed physical conditions are sufficient.

## Recommended next scholarly product

Write a **constructive process-subject paper**, not an Owen-centered response.
Its exact contribution should be:

> A physicalist identity theory on which the experiential subject is one
> temporally continuous process, distinguished from Strawson's episode-bound
> thin subject by process-genidentity conditions that permit content and
> participant change but not genuine cessation.

The paper should acknowledge Strawson in the opening literature frame, use
Taylor and Steward to formulate the bearer and persistence objections, and use
Seibt/Nicholson/Pradeu to build the diachronic account. Owen is best used as a
motivation and application: consciousness science needs an explicit subject
ontology, but that point does not organize the constructive argument.

The paper decision is warranted only if the first technical section can state
the subject–process identity and persistence conditions without the ambiguous
verb “constitutes,” without using consciousness to define the physical
process, and with a worked fission/overlap case.
