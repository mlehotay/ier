# Philosophy of Mind for Developing IER

## A lightly annotated bibliography for the v10.11.5 research programme

**Designed for:** the author and readers of Informational Experiential Realism (IER)  
**Primary research programme:** the identification problem and the continuing process-subject theory  
**Version:** IER v10.11.5  
**Role:** public reading guide and research orientation  
**Status:** non-canonical and canon-constrained  
**Date:** 8 September 2026  
**Supersedes:** the v10.11.4 milestone-based curriculum

## Status and scope

This document is a reading guide, not a second statement of IER and not a set
of exercises for the reader. It identifies the public philosophical literature
needed to understand, evaluate, and develop the IER paper programme. Its
annotations explain why each source matters, what question it helps answer, and
what it does not establish for IER.

The guide is non-canonical. Rival theories, objections, historical positions,
and speculative possibilities described here are not IER commitments. Current
IER commitments must be checked against the specification, canon, theory,
registered definitions, and other governing sources for the relevant version.

The v10.11.5 revision reflects a major change in the project's scholarly
orientation. Galen Strawson is now recognized as the explicit precedent for a
physical process-subject identity claim. IER cannot claim novelty for the bare
thesis that an experience, its subject, and a physical process are numerically
identical. Its prospective contribution is narrower: a locally bounded,
history-bearing process-subject whose numerical identity can persist through
changes of content, organization, boundary, and material participation while
ending at genuine operational cessation.

## The paper architecture this guide supports

### Paper 00: the identification problem

*The Identification Problem for Physicalist Theories of Experience* asks what
would justify treating an experiential description and a description of a
particular physical occurrence as descriptions of one thing. Physicalism does
not select the physical relatum, and choosing identity over correlation,
causation, realization, or constitution does not by itself establish
co-reference.

Paper 00 should remain the accessible methodological entry to the programme.
It needs classical identity theory, physicalism, dependence relations,
abduction, phenomenal concepts, system individuation, and standards of
empirical evidence. Strawson matters here as proof that the process-subject
candidate has a serious precedent, but Paper 00 should not reproduce the full
Strawsonian or process-persistence argument.

### Paper 01: the continuing process-subject

The mature form of Paper 01 is provisionally titled *From Thin Subjects to
Continuing Process-Subjects: Physical Identity, Unity, and Persistence after
Strawson*. It accepts Strawson's insight that the subject is not an additional
bearer behind experience, while rejecting his episode-bound and part-essential
criterion of thin-subject identity. It proposes that one continuously
operating Unified Experiential Field (UEF) is numerically identical with one
total experience-as-process and one experiential subject.

Paper 01 therefore requires literature on subject-experience identity,
panpsychism and emergence, phenomenal combination, process individuation,
material turnover, fission and fusion, interruption and reconstruction,
structural realism, and Newman's objection.

### Papers 03 and 04

The architectural-ethics paper remains downstream of the metaphysical theory
but should be intelligible conditionally, without requiring the reader to
accept IER. The comparative-review paper remains responsible for assessing
structural theories by standards also applied to IER. Both papers must be
updated when the revised identity, precedent, novelty, or subject-count claims
alter their premises.

## How to use the bibliography

The labels indicate function rather than mandatory coursework.

- **Gateway** sources map a problem-space or stabilize public vocabulary.
- **Core** sources bear directly on Paper 00 or Paper 01 and should normally be
  read in full or in the indicated sections.
- **Counterpoint** sources state a serious rival, objection, or limiting case.
- **Extension** sources broaden comparison or become important when a specific
  issue is being developed.

The shortest route to Paper 00 is listed near the end. The shortest route to
Paper 01 follows it. Readers seeking general field literacy can instead move
through the parts in order.

# Part I. Metaphysical and methodological foundations

## 1. Argument, abduction, and theoretical concepts

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Abduction”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/>

Introduces inference to the best explanation and the familiar problems of
explanatory virtue, underdetermination, and confirmation. It supplies the
general form of IER's positive identity argument: identity is proposed as the
best explanation of convergent token-level correspondence, not deduced from a
definition of experience.

### Peter Lipton, *Inference to the Best Explanation*

**Core.** Chapters 3–4 are especially useful.

Lipton clarifies how explanatory considerations can support a hypothesis
without converting parsimony into proof. Paper 00 needs this restraint because
shared boundaries, duration, organization, and causal role may favor identity
while still leaving sophisticated realization or constitution accounts open.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Concepts”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/>

Useful for separating a theoretical construct from the phenomenon it is meant
to identify. The relevance to IER is defensive: *UEF* cannot mean merely
“whatever physical system is conscious” without making the identification
claim circular.

### Jim Pryor, “Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper”

**Extension.** <https://www.jimpryor.net/teaching/guidelines/writing.html>

A compact guide to explicit arguments, objections, and dialectical structure.
It remains useful for turning the broad IER corpus into papers with bounded
claims and visible burdens of proof.

## 2. Identity, constitution, realization, and grounding

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Identity”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity/>

Provides the logical and metaphysical background for numerical identity,
indiscernibility, and identity over time. It helps prevent a biconditional,
coextension, constitution relation, or reliable correlation from being written
as though it were `A = B`.

### Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*

**Core.** Lecture III, especially the discussion of psychophysical identity.

Kripke supplies the strongest classical modal pressure on mind-brain identity.
Any IER identity claim must distinguish the epistemic conceivability of
experience without a UEF from a genuine metaphysical possibility and must not
assume that theoretical identification automatically answers the modal
objection.

### David Lewis, “An Argument for the Identity Theory”

**Extension.** <https://doi.org/10.2307/2024524>

Lewis develops a functional-definition route to identity. It is useful both as
a positive identity strategy and as a contrast with IER, which does not want
mere role-equivalence to settle the physical realization or experiential
identity question.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Physicalism”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/physicalism/>

Maps supervenience formulations, the completeness of physics, and the
“Hempel's dilemma” family of problems. It clarifies why physicalism constrains
the ontology without selecting which physical occurrence is experience.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Metaphysical Grounding”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/grounding/>

Grounding is often used where a text has not decided among identity,
constitution, realization, and dependence. IER needs the distinction because a
claim that physical organization grounds experience is weaker and differently
typed than token numerical identity.

### Jaegwon Kim, “Multiple Realization and the Metaphysics of Reduction”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.2307/2107741>

Kim pressures simple appeals to multiple realization and asks how higher-level
kinds are individuated. The paper is relevant to whether different substrates
can instantiate the same UEF organization without turning IER into generic
functionalism.

### Jessica Wilson, “No Work for a Theory of Grounding”

**Counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174X.2014.907542>

Wilson challenges indiscriminate reliance on grounding vocabulary. For IER,
the lesson is that an unspecified dependence relation should not be allowed to
replace the harder choice among identity, constitution, realization, and
causation.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Material Constitution”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/material-constitution/>

Introduces coincidence, constitution without identity, and persistence
problems. It is directly relevant to the objection that a physical operation
might constitute an experiential subject without being numerically identical
with it.

## 3. Events, processes, continuants, and parthood

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Process Philosophy”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/process-philosophy/>

Orients the reader to views on which becoming, activity, and process have
ontological priority. It does not by itself show that a process can be an
experiential subject or provide a criterion of token persistence.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Events”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/events/>

Useful for distinguishing events from properties, facts, states, and objects.
The distinction matters whenever “experience,” “operation,” or “collapse” is
treated as a concrete occurrence rather than a property instantiated by a
separate bearer.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Properties”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties/>

Provides background for the rival proposal that phenomenal character is a
property realized by or instantiated in a physical process. IER's event-level
identity does not automatically eliminate every property-level realization
account.

### John Dupré and Daniel J. Nicholson, “A Manifesto for a Processual Philosophy of Biology”

**Core.** <https://academic.oup.com/book/27525/chapter/197490752>

Argues that living systems are better understood as stabilized processes than
as substances with activities. It supplies broad support for turnover-compatible
process persistence, but it does not identify biological activity with an
experiential subject.

### Anne Sophie Meincke, “Persons as Biological Processes”

**Extension.** <https://academic.oup.com/book/27525/chapter/197499527>

Develops a processual treatment of persons. It is helpful for distinguishing
person persistence from the narrower IER question of numerical experiential-
subject persistence.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Mereology”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mereology/>

Introduces parts, wholes, overlap, fusion, and composition. These distinctions
are indispensable once physically nested or overlapping operations are
admitted, because physical or process parthood does not itself entail
phenomenal containment or subject summation.

### Peter van Inwagen, “When Are Objects Parts?”

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2214142>

A classic formulation of the special composition question. Although directed
at objects, it sharpens the question IER must answer about when interacting
physical participants compose one continuing operation rather than an
observer-selected aggregate.

### Herbert Simon, “The Architecture of Complexity”

**Extension.** <https://doi.org/10.2307/2786547>

Simon explains near-decomposability and hierarchical organization. The paper
is a useful comparison for IER's multiscale organization, while also showing
why ordinary hierarchy or weak coupling cannot by itself establish one
subject.

# Part II. Identity theory, physicalism, and the target explanandum

## 4. Classical mind-brain identity theory

### U. T. Place, “Is Consciousness a Brain Process?” (1956)

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1956.tb00560.x>

Place presents consciousness-brain process identity as an empirical
hypothesis rather than a definition. He makes psychophysical identity
intelligible but identifies conscious states or processes, not the continuing
subject of experience.

### J. J. C. Smart, “Sensations and Brain Processes” (1959)

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2182164>

Smart argues that differences in meaning and report do not require two
occurrences. His economy argument is foundational for Paper 00, although it
does not select the correct physical relatum or settle subject identity.

### Herbert Feigl, “The ‘Mental’ and the ‘Physical’” (1958)

**Extension.** Selected sections of “The ‘Mental’ and the ‘Physical.’”

Feigl's dual-access framework is an important historical route to one event
known through different epistemic perspectives. It supports the coherence of
co-reference across first-person and scientific descriptions without proving
that a proposed pair of descriptions actually co-refers.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “The Mind/Brain Identity Theory”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-identity/>

Provides historical orientation to type identity, token identity, objections,
and successor views. It is the best short entry before Place, Smart, Feigl,
Kripke, and the IER identification problem.

## 5. Token identity, functionalism, and physicalist alternatives

### Donald Davidson, “Mental Events”

**Core.** Davidson's anomalous monism permits token identity without strict
psychophysical laws connecting mental and physical kinds. The paper helps
separate a token physicalism from the stronger claim that IER has correctly
individuated the token physical process.

### Jaegwon Kim, “The Myth of Nonreductive Materialism”

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/3130081>

Kim's causal-exclusion pressure explains why an independently efficacious
phenomenal occurrence threatens duplication if the physical cause is already
complete. IER uses this pressure comparatively, not as a deductive proof of
identity.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Mental Causation”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mental-causation/>

Maps exclusion, overdetermination, and causal relevance. It clarifies the
explanatory attraction of identifying experience with the physical operation
rather than adding a downstream phenomenal product.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Functionalism”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/functionalism/>

Functionalism provides a principal rival method of individuating mental states
by causal role. IER accepts organizational relevance but denies that coarse
functional equivalence alone establishes token physical or experiential
identity.

### Hilary Putnam, “The Nature of Mental States”

**Core.** Putnam supplies the canonical multiple-realizability challenge to
simple type identity. The paper is useful for asking what must be preserved
across possible UEF realizations: generic role, causal-modal organization, or
concrete physical structure.

### Ned Block, “Troubles with Functionalism”

**Counterpoint.** <https://conservancy.umn.edu/bitstreams/0f0b9819-ce7c-4f24-ae9e-8da0e588949c/download>

Block's cases separate functional organization from phenomenal consciousness.
They caution against treating a formal or computational match as sufficient
for experience.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Multiple Realizability”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/multiple-realizability/>

Distinguishes several forms of the multiple-realizability thesis. It is useful
for preventing IER's organizational identity claim from sliding between
substrate variation, functional equivalence, and type individuation.

### Daniel Stoljar, “Two Conceptions of the Physical”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00056.x>

Stoljar distinguishes theory-based and object-based conceptions of the
physical. His analysis bears on whether Strawsonian realistic physicalism and
IER's causal-modal physicalism are using the same notion of the physical.

## 6. Phenomenal consciousness and the explanatory gap

### Thomas Nagel, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2183914>

Nagel isolates subjective character and the limits of objective description.
IER must respect the target without turning epistemic difference into a second
ontological relatum or pretending that structural description already derives
particular qualitative character.

### Ned Block, “On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00038188>

Block distinguishes phenomenal from access consciousness. This is necessary
for IER's claim that experience need not entail cognition, reportability,
intelligence, or flexible control.

### Tim Bayne, “Conscious States and Conscious Creatures”

**Extension.** <https://philpapers.org/archive/BAYCSA.pdf>

Bayne separates the consciousness of states from the consciousness of
creatures. The distinction helps expose the unfilled step between a conscious
state identity and an ontology of the subject that has or is that state.

### Joseph Levine, “Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0114.1983.tb00207.x>

Levine gives the explanatory gap its canonical form. Paper 00 distinguishes
this question—why the physical occurrence is experience at all—from the prior
identification question of which occurrence is proposed.

### Frank Jackson, “Epiphenomenal Qualia”

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2960077>

Jackson's knowledge argument pressures physical completeness and reductive
explanation. Even readers who accept later physicalist replies should retain
the distinction between possessing all physical information and occupying a
token experience.

### David Chalmers, “Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness”

**Core.** <https://consc.net/papers/facing.html>

Chalmers distinguishes functional “easy problems” from the hard problem of
experience. IER's process organization must not be presented merely as another
account of access, discrimination, report, or control.

### David Papineau, “Phenomenal and Perceptual Concepts”

**Core.** Papineau develops the idea that distinctive phenomenal concepts can
explain persistent epistemic resistance to psychophysical identity. The
strategy helps defend identity from conceivability-based objections but does
not by itself identify the physical relatum.

### Katalin Balog, “Conceivability, Possibility, and the Mind–Body Problem”

**Core.** <https://consc.net/event/neh/papers/balog.htm>

Balog offers a phenomenal-concept response to modal anti-physicalist
arguments. The paper is important for distinguishing an explanation of why
identity appears conceivably false from positive evidence that a particular
identity is true.

### David Chalmers, “Phenomenal Concepts and the Explanatory Gap”

**Counterpoint.** <https://consc.net/papers/pceg.html>

Chalmers argues that phenomenal-concept strategies may not close the
explanatory gap. IER should therefore present its qualitative correspondence
programme as unresolved rather than treating conceptual duality as a complete
answer.

## 7. Representation and structural correspondence

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Consciousness and Intentionality”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-intentionality/>

Maps relations between phenomenal character and intentional content. It helps
keep experience, content, representation, and the subject from being treated
as automatically identical relata.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Representational Theories of Consciousness”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-representational/>

Provides orientation to theories that explain phenomenal character in terms of
representational content. IER treats representation as a possible organization
within experience, not as a sufficient condition for an experiencer.

### Fred Dretske, “Experience as Representation”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1111/1533-6077.00005>

Dretske provides a strong representationalist alternative. The paper sharpens
the question whether experiential content can be explained by a representation
without first identifying what physically bears or is the total experience.

### Charles Travis, “The Silence of the Senses”

**Counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/113.449.57>

Travis rejects the assumption that perception has representational content in
the relevant sense. The dispute keeps IER from relying on “representation” as
a neutral description of every experiential difference.

# Part III. Subjects, unity, ownership, and time

## 8. The bearer ladder and conscious subject

The term *bearer* can express several different commitments. This guide retains
the following ladder as a vocabulary aid rather than an exercise:

| Level | Claim | What it does not yet establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B0 — Attribution | An experience is of or for some subject. | Introspective self-representation. |
| B1 — Phenomenal subjectivity | Experience has for-me-ness or first-personal givenness. | A substantial self, agency, or explicit mineness. |
| B2 — Synchronic unity | Several contents belong to one total experience or subject at a time. | That subject unity explains phenomenal unity rather than the reverse. |
| B3 — Ontological bearer | Something instantiates, undergoes, or is the subject of experience. | That this something is distinct from organized experience. |
| B4 — Diachronic bearer | One numerically identical subject persists through changing experiences. | A simple, indivisible, or immaterial substance. |
| B5 — Substantial bearer | Unity is explained by a metaphysically simple subject or substance. | That organism, process, constitution, bundle, or no-self views fail. |

### Matthew Owen, “The Neglected Conscious Subject in Consciousness Science”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.06.009>

Owen argues that consciousness science often theorizes conscious states while
leaving the subject undertheorized. This motivates the IER project but does not
determine whether the subject is an organism, substance, capacity, process, or
experiential whole.

### Matthew Owen, Darren Hight, and Anthony G. Hudetz, “Human Brain Organoids and the Mereological Fallacy” (2025)

**Core comparison.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s12152-024-09577-y>

Owen and colleagues argue against attributing organism-level properties to
brain organoids merely because they contain relevant neural activity. The
paper sharpens the mereological and bearer questions for consciousness
science, although its organism-centered caution does not decide whether a
properly individuated physical process could itself be a subject.

### Raphaël Millière, “The Varieties of Selflessness”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.33735/phimisci.2020.I.48>

Millière distinguishes different losses or absences associated with selfhood.
The distinctions help separate minimal subjectivity from self-modeling,
narrative identity, agency, bodily self-representation, and explicit mineness.

### E. J. Lowe, *Subjects of Experience*

**Counterpoint.** Introduction and chapters 1–2.

Lowe offers a substantial-subject alternative. His work is valuable precisely
because it refuses to let unity and ownership be absorbed into an impersonal
process without an account of what makes that process a bearer.

### Eric Olson, *What Are We?*

**Counterpoint.** Chapters 1–3.

Olson's animalism identifies us with organisms rather than experiences,
brains, selves, or psychological continuities. It keeps organism persistence
distinct from IER's proposed experiential-subject persistence.

### Barry Dainton, “Selfhood and the Flow of Experience” (2012)

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1163/9789401207904_009>

Dainton takes Strawson's episode-sized subjects seriously but argues that the
continuity of experience favors a more persistent subject. His view is a near
precedent for IER's diachronic concern, although he moves toward an experiential
capacity rather than identifying the subject with the currently operating
physical process.

### Barry Dainton, “I—The Sense of Self” (2016)

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1093/arisup/akw007>

Dainton characterizes subjects through capacities for unified experience and
treats experiences as produced by those subjects. This offers persistence
through unconsciousness at the cost of reintroducing a bearer-production
relation that IER rejects.

### Henry Taylor, “The Relation Between Subjects and Their Conscious Experiences” (2020)

**Counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-019-01379-w>

Taylor defends a property-exemplification account on which a subject partly
constitutes an experience but is not identical with it. He treats Strawson's
identity view as the prominent revisionary alternative and directly pressures
the claim that an episode or process can replace the bearer.

### Helen Steward, “Substances, Agents and Processes” (2020)

**Counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1017/S0031819119000494>

Steward argues that processes are activities of enduring agents or organisms
rather than replacements for them. Her objection is among the clearest tests
of whether IER has genuinely explained subjecthood or merely redescribed the
subject's activity.

### William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, “The Stream of Thought”

**Extension.** <https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/>

James allows the passing thought to perform much of the appropriative work
otherwise assigned to an ego. He is an important conceptual antecedent to
anti-substantival subject accounts, but not a physical process-subject identity
theorist.

### Evan Thompson, “Précis of *Waking, Dreaming, Being*” (2016)

**Extension.** <https://philpapers.org/rec/THOPOW>

Thompson describes selfhood as enacted and processual. The position is close in
spirit but does not supply the strict identity of a particular physical
operation with the total experience and subject.

## 9. Unity, for-me-ness, and ownership

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “The Unity of Consciousness”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-unity/>

Surveys phenomenal, subject, representational, access, spatial, and temporal
unity. It is a necessary safeguard against inferring one subject directly from
one measure of physical integration.

### Tim Bayne and David Chalmers, “What Is the Unity of Consciousness?”

**Core.** <https://academic.oup.com/book/34703/chapter/296340753>

Bayne and Chalmers distinguish subject unity from phenomenal unity and define
several composition relations among experiences. Their analysis shows that a
physical unity condition still needs a bridge to phenomenal and subject unity.

### Luke Roelofs, “The Unity of Consciousness, Within Subjects and Between Subjects”

**Extension.** <https://philarchive.org/archive/ROETUO-2>

Roelofs connects unity within a subject to combination between subjects. The
paper is particularly useful for testing IER's claim that physical overlap and
parthood do not entail phenomenal combination.

### Luke Roelofs, “Overlapping Minds and the Hedonic Calculus” (2024)

**Core counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-024-02167-x>

Roelofs develops the ethical and metaphysical pressure created by overlapping
candidate minds. The paper directly tests whether IER's regime-relative
boundaries and incompatible-ownership condition can distinguish genuine
overlap from duplicate description or arbitrary proliferation.

### Eric Schwitzgebel and Sophie R. Nelson, “When Counting Conscious Subjects, the Result Needn't Always Be a Determinate Whole Number” (2026)

**Core counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.1080/09515089.2025.2520364>

Schwitzgebel and Nelson argue that naturalistic consciousness theories may
permit fractional or indeterminate subject counts. Their challenge is aimed
at IER's categorical one-operation/one-subject commitment: physical
indeterminacy about whether a boundary condition obtains must be separated
from the stronger claim that subjecthood itself is fractional.

### Marie Guillot, “I Me Mine”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-016-0313-4>

Guillot separates for-me-ness, me-ness, and mineness. These distinctions limit
what IER may claim when translating structural ownership into phenomenal
subjectivity.

### Dan Zahavi and Uriah Kriegel, “For-Me-Ness: What It Is and What It Is Not”

**Core.** <https://philarchive.org/rec/ZAHFWI>

Zahavi and Kriegel defend minimal for-me-ness while separating it from
reflection and explicit self-representation. Their account is a central
phenomenological comparison for IER's “ownership without an owner.”

### Thomas Metzinger, *Being No One*

**Counterpoint.** The précis and conclusion provide the shortest route.

Metzinger rejects a substantial self while explaining selfhood through a
transparent self-model. IER agrees that no inner self-substance is required but
does not identify the experiential subject with a self-representation.

## 10. Personal identity and difficult cases

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Personal Identity”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/>

Surveys bodily, psychological, biological, and reductionist accounts. It helps
keep person identity separate from the numerical identity of an occurrent
experiential subject.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Temporal Consciousness”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-temporal/>

Introduces retention, temporal extension, succession, and the specious
present. It provides phenomenological constraints on any account that treats
experience as a temporally extended process.

### Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*, Part III

**Core.** Parfit's fission, teletransportation, and reductionist arguments
separate what matters in survival from strict numerical identity. IER adopts a
strict nonbranching identity condition, so Parfit is the natural counterpoint
when psychological and causal continuity survive without one continuing
process-subject.

### Elizabeth Schechter, *Self-Consciousness and “Split” Brains*

**Core.** Introduction and chapters 4–5.

Schechter shows how split-brain cases destabilize simple assumptions about
subject count and unity. Her work is important for any physical boundary rule
that purports to distinguish one continuing subject from two.

### Ian Phillips, “The Temporal Structure of Experience”

**Extension.** Phillips's work sharpens the debate over whether experience is
itself temporally extended or merely represents temporal extension. This bears
directly on the temporal grain of the IER identity relatum.

### David B. Hershenov, “The Metaphysical Problem of Intermittent Existence and the Possibility of Resurrection” (2003)

**Counterpoint.** <https://doi.org/10.5840/faithphil200320127> · [Author
manuscript](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~dh25/articles/TheMetaphysicalProblemofIntermittentExistence.pdf)

Hershenov permits an organism to exist again after a gap under demanding
material conditions. The contrast clarifies IER's opposite commitment: if the
subject is the occurrent process rather than the organism or its matter,
genuine cessation ends that subject even when an exact reconstruction later
occurs.

# Part IV. Strawson and the explicit process-subject precedent

## 11. The central identity text

### Galen Strawson, “What Is the Relation Between an Experience, the Subject of the Experience, and the Content of the Experience?” (2003)

**Core.** *Philosophical Issues* 13: 279–315.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1533-6077.00015)

This is the exact precedent for IER's identity claim. Strawson argues that an
experience, its thin subject, its content, and a bounded physical synergy can
be one process-stuff occurrence. IER inherits the rejection of an additional
bearer but departs from Strawson's part-essential, episode-bound criterion of
token identity.

The paper should be read before claims are made about the novelty of physical
process-subject identity. It also supplies the cleanest formulation of the
question that now organizes Paper 01: can the subject be the experience while
remaining numerically identical through extensive change?

## 12. Real materialism, realistic monism, and panpsychism

### Galen Strawson, “Real Materialism” (2003)

**Core.** In Louise M. Antony and Norbert Hornstein (eds.), *Chomsky and His
Critics*, 49–88. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470690024.ch3)

Strawson argues that experiential reality must be included within a genuinely
realistic materialism rather than excluded by a thin conception of matter. The
paper is necessary background for understanding why his physicalism does not
resemble standard reductive materialism.

### Galen Strawson, “Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism” (2006)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 3–31. [Open
copy](https://informationphilosopher.com/solutions/philosophers/strawsong/Strawson_JCS_Why_Panpsychism.pdf)

Strawson argues from the reality of experience, physicalism, and the rejection
of brute radical emergence to experientiality at the fundamental physical
level. IER accepts complete physical determination and rejects strong
emergence, but denies that macroexperience must inherit experiential
constituents.

### David Papineau, “Comments on Galen Strawson: ‘Realistic Monism’” (2006)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 100–109. [Author

Papineau argues that a complex system-level physical condition may simply be
identical with consciousness even if its constituents are not conscious. This
blocks any claim that IER originates non-panpsychist system-level identity and
supplies a direct alternative to Strawson's constituent-inheritance inference.

### Galen Strawson, “Panpsychism? Reply to Commentators with a Celebration of Descartes” (2006)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 184–280.
[Record](https://philpapers.org/rec/STRPRT)

Strawson clarifies and defends realistic monism against the symposium
responses. The reply is needed to avoid attributing a simplistic “everything
has a mind” view to him and to see which anti-emergence premises IER accepts,
rejects, or reinterprets.

### Frank Jackson, “Galen Strawson on Panpsychism” (2006)

**Counterpoint.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 62–64.
[Record](https://philpapers.org/rec/JACGSO)

Jackson questions the force of Strawson's inference. The short commentary is
useful for identifying where the emergence argument relies on substantive
assumptions about the nature of the physical rather than physicalism alone.

### William Seager, “The ‘Intrinsic Nature’ Argument for Panpsychism” (2006)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 129–145. [Author
copy](https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/~seager/intnat.pdf)

Seager isolates the intrinsic-nature argument from broader anti-emergence
considerations. This matters because IER's *intrinsic constraint* is a
causal-modal relation and should not be confused with Strawson's or Russellian
claims about the categorical intrinsic nature of matter.

### Sam Coleman, “Being Realistic: Why Physicalism May Entail Panexperientialism” (2006)

**Extension.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 40–52.
[Record](https://philpapers.org/rec/COLBR)

Coleman develops a sympathetic but discriminating response to Strawson. The
paper helps locate the move from experience as unquestionably real to a wider
distribution of experiential being.

### Philip Goff, “Experiences Don't Sum” (2006)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11): 53–61. [Open
copy](https://www.organism.earth/library/document/experiences-dont-sum)

Goff argues that the presence of microexperiences does not explain a unified
macroexperience. The paper supplies an early statement of the combination
pressure that IER avoids by denying phenomenal constituent inheritance.

### Galen Strawson, “There Is No Mystery of Consciousness, and the Demand for Explanation Begs the Question” (2026)

**Core.** *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 33.

Strawson's current statement treats experience as wholly natural physical
reality and resists the demand to derive it from a supposedly nonexperiential
base. It is important for representing his mature position rather than
criticizing only the 2006 formulation.

### David Papineau, *Thinking about Consciousness* (2002)

**Core.** Selected chapters on identity physicalism, the explanatory gap, and
phenomenal concepts.

Papineau provides the most developed background for the system-level identity
alternative invoked in his reply to Strawson. His treatment supports the
possibility of identity without experiential microconstituents while leaving
open which physical condition is the correct identity relatum.

# Part V. Emergence and the combination problem

## 13. Emergence

### David J. Chalmers, “Strong and Weak Emergence” (2006)

**Core.** [Open copy](https://consc.net/papers/emergence.pdf)

Chalmers distinguishes weak emergence, compatible with derivability in
principle, from strong emergence involving additional fundamental principles.
IER should reject strong psychophysical emergence while allowing that a
system-level process may remain predictively or explanatorily emergent.

### James Van Cleve, “Mind—Dust or Magic? Panpsychism Versus Emergence” (1990)

**Core.** <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2214193>

Van Cleve presents the apparent dilemma between experiential ingredients and
brute emergence. IER's response is to reject the inference from complete
physical determination to experiential constituent inheritance, not to deny
that the macrooperation has physical constituents.

### Peter Carruthers and Elizabeth Schechter, “Can Panpsychism Bridge the Explanatory Gap?” (2006)

**Counterpoint.** [Record](https://philpapers.org/rec/CARCPB-2)

Carruthers and Schechter question whether panpsychism improves the explanatory
situation. The paper supports a key limitation on IER's anti-panpsychist
argument: avoiding microexperience aggregation does not by itself complete the
positive qualitative correspondence account.

## 14. Combination, composition, and subject summing

### Sam Coleman, “The Real Combination Problem: Panpsychism, Micro-Subjects, and Emergence” (2014)

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s10670-013-9431-x>

Coleman argues that the deepest combination problem concerns subjects rather
than qualities alone. The paper is central to IER's distinction between
physical composition and phenomenal or subject composition.

### David J. Chalmers, “The Combination Problem for Panpsychism” (2017)

**Core.** [Open copy](https://consc.net/papers/combination.pdf)

Chalmers provides the most useful taxonomy of subject-summing, quality,
structure, and unity problems. IER should say that it avoids the constitutive
panpsychist problem by rejecting phenomenal inheritance; it does not solve the
problem on panpsychist premises.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Panpsychism”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/panpsychism/>

Provides orientation to constitutive and nonconstitutive forms,
panprotopsychism, cosmopsychism, and combination. This breadth prevents an
argument against experiential microconstituents from being advertised as a
refutation of every panpsychist theory.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Russellian Monism”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russellian-monism/>

Maps the view that physics describes dispositional or structural roles while
leaving intrinsic nature open. It states the most direct challenge to IER's
claim that concrete causal-modal structure is an adequate physical identity
relatum.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Neutral Monism”

**Extension.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/neutral-monism/>

Neutral monism offers a common-basis alternative to both standard materialism
and panpsychism. It broadens the comparison set without directly supporting
the IER process-subject identity.

# Part VI. Process individuation and persistence

## 15. Genidentity and constitutive process continuity

### Thomas Pradeu, “Genidentity and Biological Processes” (2018)

**Core.** In Nicholson and Dupré (eds.), *Everything Flows*, 96–112.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0005)

Pradeu develops genidentity as a way of following an individual through
temporally ordered, causally connected transformations. It supplies the
historical form of continuity IER needs to permit material turnover without
reducing identity to resemblance.

### James DiFrisco, “Biological Processes: Criteria of Identity and Persistence” (2018)

**Core.** In Nicholson and Dupré (eds.), *Everything Flows*, 76–95.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0004)

DiFrisco distinguishes stage individuation from diachronic persistence and
compares causal with spatiotemporal criteria. The paper helps IER separate the
question “is this one process now?” from “is this the same process later?”

### Alexandre Guay and Thomas Pradeu, “To Be Continued: The Genidentity of Physical and Biological Processes” (2015)

**Core.** In *Individuals Across the Sciences*, 317–347.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199382514.003.0017)

Guay and Pradeu extend genidentity across physical and biological cases. Their
account is a major resource for making process continuity causal and historical
rather than dependent on a fixed inventory of matter.

### Johanna Seibt, “Ontological Tools for the Process Turn in Biology” (2018)

**Core.** In Nicholson and Dupré (eds.), *Everything Flows*, 113–136.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0006)

Seibt distinguishes several dimensions of process individuation, including
spatiotemporal profile, participant structure, dynamic composition, dynamic
shape, and context. Her framework is the best general warning against asking
one undifferentiated criterion to settle boundary, grain, and persistence at
once.

### Daniel J. Nicholson, “Reconceptualizing the Organism: From Complex Machine to Flowing Stream” (2018)

**Core.** In Nicholson and Dupré (eds.), *Everything Flows*, 139–166.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780198779636.003.0007)

Nicholson treats organisms as dynamically self-maintaining streams whose
identity can survive the replacement of matter. His account supports the
possibility of turnover-compatible process persistence while leaving the
experiential identity and no-gap conditions to IER.

### Helen Steward, “Processes, Continuants, and Individuals” (2013)

**Core.** *Mind* 122(487): 781–812.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzt080)

Steward argues that processes and continuants are different ontological
categories and resists replacing agents with their activities. Her view is a
foundational objection to the IER proposal, not merely a vocabulary dispute.

### Rowland Stout, “The Category of Occurrent Continuants” (2016)

**Core.** *Mind* 125(497): 41–62.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/fzv138)

Stout develops the category of an occurrent continuant: something whose
continued existence consists in ongoing activity. This is especially close to
IER's attempt to make the operation, rather than a dormant capacity, the
persisting bearer.

## 16. Boundary, turnover, change, and branching

### Antony Galton and Riichiro Mizoguchi, “The Water Falls but the Waterfall Does Not Fall” (2009)

**Core.** *Applied Ontology* 4: 71–107.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.3233/AO-2009-0067)

The waterfall case clarifies how a process can remain numerically identifiable
through complete turnover of participating matter. The authors' distinctions
help prevent the subject's identity from being assigned either to the water or
to an abstract type detached from the concrete occurrence.

### John Collier, “Self-Organization, Individuation and Identity” (2004)

**Core.** *Revue internationale de philosophie* 228(2): 151–172.
[Source](https://shs.cairn.info/journal-revue-internationale-de-philosophie-2004-2-page-151?lang=en)

Collier links self-organization with individuation and persistence. His work
supports a dynamically maintained boundary but also shows that graded cohesion
cannot simply be stipulated as a categorical subject threshold.

### Anne Sophie Meincke, “The Disappearance of Change: Towards a Process Account of Persistence” (2019)

**Core.** *International Journal of Philosophical Studies* 27(1): 12–30.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/09672559.2018.1548634)

Meincke argues that substance ontology makes genuine change difficult and that
process persistence can accommodate transformation internally. This supports
the possibility that extensive reorganization alters one subject without
replacing it.

### Anne Sophie Meincke, “One or Two? A Process View of Pregnancy” (2022)

**Core.** *Philosophical Studies* 179: 1495–1521.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11098-021-01716-y)

Pregnancy supplies a difficult case of incorporation, gradual differentiation,
asymmetric separation, and changing boundaries. The paper is particularly
useful for thinking about fission without assuming that identity must branch.

### Christopher J. Austin, “Organisms, Activity, and Being: On the Substance of Process Ontology” (2020)

**Core.** *European Journal for Philosophy of Science* 10: article 13.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13194-020-0278-0)

Austin develops a sophisticated relation between organisms and the activities
constitutive of them. The paper helps IER distinguish “the operation is the
subject” from weaker claims that activity constitutes or sustains a persisting
substance.

## 17. Supporting process and organizational literature

### Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, *Autopoiesis and Cognition*

**Extension.** Selected chapters.

Autopoiesis provides a canonical account of operationally closed,
self-producing organization. It supports analysis of autonomous process
boundaries but does not make every autopoietic system conscious.

### Evan Thompson, *Mind in Life*

**Extension.** Chapters on autonomy and the life–mind continuity thesis.

Thompson integrates enactivism, embodiment, and dynamic organization. The work
is a close conceptual neighbor while leaving open the strict identity relation
and the possibility of nonliving UEF realizations.

### Maël Montévil and Matteo Mossio, “Biological Organisation as Closure of Constraints”

**Core.** <https://doi.org/10.1007/s11625-014-0284-7>

Montévil and Mossio explain biological organization through mutually dependent
constraints. Their framework is useful for defining closure physically, while
IER must still distinguish ordinary organizational closure from the stronger
nonfactorizable UEF condition.

# Part VII. Concrete structure and Newman's objection

## 18. The anti-triviality problem

### M. H. A. Newman, “Mr. Russell's ‘Causal Theory of Perception’” (1928)

**Core.** *Mind* 37(146): 137–148.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/mind/XXXVII.146.137)

Newman argues that a purely structural claim can become nearly trivial: given
the right cardinality, relations can be imposed on a domain to realize almost
any abstract pattern. IER faces this objection if a desired UEF structure is
selected only after the supposed subject has been identified.

### Peter M. Ainsworth, “Newman's Objection” (2009)

**Core.** *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* 60(1): 135–171.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/bjps/axn051)

Ainsworth provides the most careful modern analysis of the objection and the
possible role of privileged or natural relations. For IER, every appeal to
physical privilege, modality, or naturalness must receive an empirical and
counterfactual interpretation rather than remain a label.

### Michael Esfeld, “The Modal Nature of Structures in Ontic Structural Realism” (2009)

**Core.** *International Studies in the Philosophy of Science* 23(2):
179–194. [Open record](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/1580/)

Esfeld treats physical structure as modal and causally consequential rather
than merely extensional. This supports IER's attempt to individuate a process
through what it can and cannot physically do next under intervention and
counterfactual variation.

### Stathis Psillos, “Is Structural Realism the Best of Both Worlds?” (1995)

**Counterpoint.** *Dialectica* 49(1): 15–46.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1746-8361.1995.tb00113.x)

Psillos argues that structure cannot be cleanly detached from the nature of
the physical entities and relations that instantiate it. His criticism blocks
an IER account based on an empty graph or isomorphism while remaining
compatible with concrete causal roles and powers.

### Majid Davoody Beni, “Structural Realist Account of the Self” (2016)

**Core.** *Synthese* 193: 3727–3740.
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-016-1098-9)

Beni offers prior art for an anti-substantival structural self organized by an
information-processing pattern. His proposal concerns the unification of
self-concepts and cortical midline activity, not the strict identity of the
total experiential bearer with a history-sensitive physical operation.

## 19. Information, models, and physical interpretation

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Information”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/information/>

Distinguishes Shannon, semantic, biological, and other notions of information.
IER requires a physical constraint interpretation and should not infer
experience from information-bearing or computation alone.

### Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Causal Models”

**Gateway.** <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/causal-models/>

Provides background on interventions, counterfactuals, variables, and causal
graphs. These are needed to make the proposed typed domain and bilateral
participation boundary more than an observer-fitted structural description.

### Larissa Albantakis et al., “Integrated Information Theory (IIT) 4.0”

**Core comparison.** <https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011465>

IIT supplies a leading attempt to formulate phenomenal existence in terms of
intrinsic physical structure, system cuts, and exclusion. It is an essential
comparison for IER's nonfactorization, boundary, scale, and subject-count
claims, although the theories use different primitives and identity arguments.

### Nihat Ay, work on information geometry and stochastic interaction

**Extension.** Ay's formal work is relevant to factorization, interaction, and
multiscale decomposition. It becomes important when IER's qualitative
nonfactorization claims are converted into explicit mathematical tests.

# Part VIII. Consciousness science and levels of evidence

## 20. Contemporary theory comparison

### Anil Seth and Tim Bayne, “Theories of Consciousness” (2022)

**Gateway.** <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-022-00587-4>

Surveys leading neuroscientific theories and clarifies their explanatory
targets. It helps distinguish a theory of access, broadcast, recurrence,
prediction, or integration from a theory of what the experiential subject is.

### Cogitate Consortium et al., “Adversarial Testing of Global Neuronal Workspace and Integrated Information Theories” (2025)

**Core empirical comparison.** <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1>

This large adversarial collaboration illustrates how theory-derived empirical
predictions can be preregistered and compared. It is methodological guidance
for IER, not direct evidence for or against a UEF identity.

### Camilo Miguel Signorelli, Ignacio Cea, and Robert Prentner, “We Need to Compare Theories of Consciousness” (2025)

**Core methodological comparison.** <https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niaf034>

The paper emphasizes explicit comparison among theories and explanatory
dimensions. Its relevance is strongest for Paper 04 and for preventing IER's
vocabulary from being evaluated by different standards than neighboring
theories.

### George Mashour et al., “Conscious Processing and the Global Neuronal Workspace Hypothesis” (2020)

**Extension.** <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2020.01.026>

Global neuronal workspace theory explains broadcast and conscious access. It
is a useful contrast because global availability may be evidence about a
system while remaining distinct from the physical occurrence proposed as the
total experience and subject.

## 21. Evidence and epistemic limits

The empirical literature should be read with four levels kept separate:

1. evidence that a physical model is dynamically adequate;
2. evidence that a concrete process has been non-circularly individuated;
3. evidence that its boundary, duration, internal changes, and counterfactuals
   correspond to those of experience;
4. warrant for numerical psychophysical identity.

Correlation, report, intervention, anatomical necessity, functional role, and
formal integration can contribute to this sequence without independently
settling the final relation. The current IER programme supplies a metaphysical
and formal schema, not a validated consciousness detector.

# Part X. Reading IER through the public literature

## 24. IER sources and their roles

### The IER seed-book

The published seed-book is the v10.10.7 statement of the theory's identity and
structural architecture. It should be treated as a versioned public artifact,
not silently rewritten to reflect later literature findings.

### Paper 00, *The Identification Problem for Physicalist Theories of Experience*

Read after Place, Smart, physicalism, dependence relations, the explanatory
gap, phenomenal concepts, and abduction. Its principal task is methodological:
to state which physical occurrence is proposed and what would warrant
co-reference rather than correlation, causation, realization, constitution,
or structural analogy.

### Paper 01, *From Thin Subjects to Continuing Process-Subjects*

Read after Strawson 2003, the 2006 realistic-monism exchange, Papineau,
combination, the subject literature, process genidentity, boundary and fission
cases, and Newman's objection. Its principal task is constructive: to defend
the continuing UEF operation as the total experience and subject while making
its physical persistence criterion independently specifiable.

### The IER specification, canon, theory, and registered definitions

These remain the governing sources for current IER commitments. Public papers
are arguments about the theory, not independent canonical amendments. Where a
paper uses *constitutes*, *realizes*, *sustains*, *bears*, or *is* differently
from the governing sources, the relation must be typed rather than harmonized
by paraphrase.

### Worked examples and the Stapled Net

The existing examples establish intelligibility and illustrate components of
the architecture. They do not yet demonstrate the complete physical
individuation schema. A sufficient worked case would fix variables, temporal
grain, intervention family, bilateral boundary, nonfactorization, successor
production, sediment inheritance, turnover, splitting, cessation, and restart
without using consciousness as an input.

## 25. The current IER synthesis supported by the Strawson research

The bibliography locates the following proposal; it does not make the proposal
canonical or prove it:

```text
one maximal continuing UEF operation
    = one total experience-as-process
    = one experiential subject
```

The proposed invariant is not a permanent material inventory, qualitative
content, psychological similarity, static geometry, or inactive capacity. It
is the uninterrupted continuation of one self-producing, history-bearing,
nonfactorizable causal-modal operation. Participant turnover and boundary
migration are permitted when mediated by the extant operation. Numerical
identity neither branches nor fuses. A genuine UEF-null interval ends the
subject; later reconstruction produces a numerically new subject even when
organism, person, memory, or first-person-seeming continuity remains.

IER accepts complete physical determination but rejects constituent
inheritance: the macroexperience's complete physical determination does not
entail that it is phenomenally composed from experiential ultimates. A
lower-scale operation, if it independently qualifies as a UEF, is another
subject; its phenomenal contents do not thereby combine with or become
available to a higher-scale subject.

The principal unresolved burdens are:

- positive warrant for identity over constitution and realization;
- non-circular physical individuation of boundaries and continuity intervals;
- a worked physical or neuroscientific instantiation of the persistence rule;
- qualitative correspondence between causal-modal organization and particular
  phenomenal character;
- empirical attribution under structural privacy;
- treatment of vague or indeterminate physical transition cases; and
- the remaining Russellian intrinsic-nature challenge.

neighbors.

## Closing orientation

The v10.11.5 reading programme no longer treats IER as entering an empty space
between classical identity theory and contemporary consciousness science.
Strawson supplies the missing explicit precedent: the subject can be the
experience and the physical process rather than an additional bearer.

The remaining research question is correspondingly sharper:

> Can one locally bounded physical operation remain numerically identical with
> one experiential subject through changing content, organization, boundary,
> and material participation, and can that continuing operation be
> individuated without presupposing consciousness or the desired subject
> count?

That question now organizes the relation between Paper 00, Paper 01, the
worked-example programme, and the wider IER corpus.
