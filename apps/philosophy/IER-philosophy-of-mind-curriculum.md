# Philosophy of Mind for Finishing IER

## A two-part curriculum in field literacy and IER research practice

Designed for: the author of Informational Experiential Realism (IER)\\
Primary project: four papers on identification, process identity, architectural ethics, and the structural-consciousness literature\\
Full route: 40 milestone-based modules, normally 5 — 7 hours each\\
Typical duration: 10 — 14 months at one module per week, with breaks between phases\\
Essential route: 20 compressed stages, listed near the end\\
Method: advance by completed outputs rather than elapsed weeks


## What this curriculum is for

The aim is not to acquire a general credential in philosophy of mind. It is to
become capable of locating, defending, revising, and eventually completing the
four-paper IER programme:

1. Paper 00 — “The Identification Problem for Physicalist Theories of
   Experience.” Why physicalism and neural correlation do not yet identify
   which physical event is experience; why a recurrently organized,
   self-maintaining constraint regime is a candidate rather than a proven
   solution.
2. Paper 01 — “A Process Identity Theory of Experience.” The positive IER
   identity claim; one UEF/one subject; ownership without an inner owner;
   frontier geometry; collapse; persistence; participation; agency; and the
   denial of nested subjects.
3. Paper 03 — “Architectural Ethics: Authorship of Phenomenology in
   Constructed Consciousness.” The moral significance of deliberately
   selecting the relatively persistent organization that structures another
   subject’s possible experience and development.
4. Paper 04 — “Constraint, Closure, and the Missing Subject.” The current
   structural-consciousness cluster; its relation to established theories;
   differences among identity, constitution, realization, emergence, and
   consciousness-first structural views; and AI-assisted philosophy as a
   research method.

The curriculum is successful when you can do four things without help:

- reconstruct the strongest argument for and against each paper;
- place every major IER claim in the correct public philosophical debate;
- distinguish what IER has argued from what it has defined, illustrated,
  formalized, proposed for testing, or merely left open; and
- explain IER to a philosopher in vocabulary that does not require prior
  acceptance of IER.

## Why the curriculum has two parts

The cleanest boundary is not “philosophy” versus “IER.” IER itself is
philosophy, and several IER concepts have close public antecedents. The useful
boundary is between two kinds of intellectual work.

### Part I — Field literacy: learn the public problem-space

Part I teaches concepts, arguments, theories, and research practices whose
meaning does not depend on IER. Its purpose is to make you an independent judge
of the theory. Exercises may mention IER only after the public argument has
been reconstructed on its own terms.

By the end of Part I, you should be able to enter a discussion of identity
theory, physicalism, subjects of experience, phenomenal unity, process
ontology, consciousness science, or artificial moral patiency without first
translating everything into constraint, UEF, collapse, or admissible futures.

### Part II — IER research apprenticeship: use that field literacy on the theory

Part II treats the four papers and the stabilized IER corpus as research
objects. It asks what each claim means, what supports it, what would displace
it, how it relates to neighboring views, and whether the papers agree with one
another.

This boundary prevents two opposite failures:

- learning the public literature only as a set of labels to attach to IER; and
- learning a large amount of philosophy without converting it into decisions
  about the four papers.

## How the four papers depend on the curriculum

| Paper | Public prerequisites from Part I | IER work in Part II | Principal unresolved burden |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Paper 00 | Identity, dependence, explanation, physicalism, events and processes, causal modeling, consciousness science | Modules 25 — 29 | Give positive reason to identify experience with this regime rather than merely treating the regime as a correlate, realization, constitution base, or structural analogy. |
| Paper 01 | Subjects, bearers, unity, ownership, process ontology, persistence, temporal consciousness, individuation | Modules 30 — 34 | Show that one UEF is one subject without presupposing subject unity in the physical definition of a UEF. |
| Paper 03 | Welfare, moral status, autonomy, non-domination, population and procreative ethics, precaution, moral uncertainty | Modules 35 — 36 | Establish what authorship adds to existing ethical categories and prevent “architecture” from expanding to mean every influence on a mind. |
| Paper 04 | Comparative theory, review method, established consciousness theories, structuralism, process and closure traditions | Modules 37 — 38 | Demonstrate a real convergence without mistaking shared vocabulary, recommendation adjacency, publication timing, or AI-assisted style for a philosophical school. |
| Four-paper programme | Argumentation, source discipline, claim typing, novelty analysis | Modules 39 — 40 | Keep identity, empirical, ethical, and historiographic claims mutually consistent while allowing the papers to have different scopes. |

Paper 00 should remain the accessible entry point. Paper 01 is the strongest
positive statement of the theory. Paper 03 should be intelligible without
accepting IER. Paper 04 should compare IER by the same standards it applies to
its neighbors.

## Study method

### The module rhythm

Use the same routine for each module:

1. Orient — 45 — 60 minutes. Read the gateway source only far enough to map
   the debate.
2. Read — 2 — 3 hours. Read the core source slowly. Optional stretch readings
   are for live problems, not completion points.
3. Reconstruct — 60 minutes. Write the strongest version of the argument in
   numbered premise — conclusion form.
4. Oppose — 30 — 45 minutes. State the strongest objection and the best reply
   available to the author.
5. Apply — 60 — 90 minutes. Complete the module output.
6. Log — 15 minutes. Add entries to `clarified`, `threatened`, `supported`,
   and `open`.

One module may take more than one week. A module is complete when its output is
good enough to show another person, not when the readings have been opened.

### Reading labels

- Gateway: orientation. Do not cite it as though it were the primary
  position.
- Core: read closely and make an argument card.
- Case: an empirical or hypothetical test used to force distinctions.
- Stretch: optional unless the module reveals that it bears directly on an
  unresolved IER claim.

### The argument card

Create one page for every core reading:

```text
Question:
Author's answer:
Key terms in the author's own vocabulary:
Argument (numbered premises and conclusion):
Strongest objection:
Best reply available to the author:
What the argument would show if sound:
What it would not show:
Relation to IER: threat / support / distinction / no direct consequence
Confidence and unresolved source questions:
```

The line “what it would not show” is mandatory. It is the best defense against
turning a useful argument into a larger conclusion than it supports.

### Six running research instruments

Maintain these throughout both parts:

1. Vocabulary ledger: public term, author-specific meaning, IER meaning,
   false friends, and permitted translation.
2. Claim ledger: exact claim, relata, relation, modal force, evidence,
   strongest rival, and status.
3. Bearer ladder: B0 — B5, defined below.
4. Case bank: ordinary systems, brains, artificial systems, interruptions,
   fission/fusion cases, and ethical construction cases.
5. Source-audit table: exact version, stable link, evidential basis, quoted
   claim, your paraphrase, and confidence.
6. Issue register: candidate clarification, argument needed, empirical task,
   formal task, possible contradiction, or proposed revision.

### Canon quarantine and stabilization

Do not change the IER specification or the public-repository selection and
staging sets merely because one reading produces pressure. The public subset is
a stabilization surface. Record candidate changes in the issue register.

A proposed change graduates from the register only after:

- the opposing argument has been reconstructed charitably;
- at least one serious reply has been read;
- the change has been checked against identity, subjecthood, unity,
  individuation, persistence, qualitative character, and ethics;
- its effect on all four papers has been stated; and
- it can be expressed without silently changing IER from identity to
  realization, constitution, emergence, or criterion.

Dead links or imperfect staging are not reasons to disturb the stabilization
surface during the curriculum.


## Part I — Field Literacy

## Phase A — Philosophical method and metaphysical tools

### Module 1 — Arguments, entailment, abduction, and conceptual engineering

Questions

- What is the difference among validity, soundness, explanatory value, and
  plausibility?
- When does an inference to the best explanation support identity rather than
  merely a useful model?
- When is a new term a legitimate theoretical construct, and when is it a
  relabeling of the target phenomenon?

Read

- Gateway: [Abduction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/) and
  [Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/), selected sections.
- Core: Peter Lipton, *Inference to the Best Explanation*, chapters 3 — 4.
- Practice: Jim Pryor, [“Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy
  Paper”](https://www.jimpryor.net/teaching/guidelines/writing.html).

Output

Take one argument from each IER paper and separate: premises, definitions,
background assumptions, inference rule, conclusion, and residual uncertainty.
Do not evaluate IER yet. The aim is to learn claim anatomy.

### Module 2 — Identity, necessity, and criteria

Questions

- What follows from `A = B` that does not follow from `A iff B`?
- What is the difference between a criterion for identifying something and the
  nature of the thing identified?
- Are true identities necessary, necessary a posteriori, or sometimes merely
  nomological?

Read

- Gateway: [Identity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity/) and
  [The Mind/Brain Identity Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-identity/), sections 1 — 2.
- Core: Saul Kripke, *Naming and Necessity*, Lecture III, especially the
  psychophysical identity discussion.
- Stretch: David Lewis, “An Argument for the Identity Theory,”
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2024524).

Output

Make a one-page table distinguishing numerical identity, qualitative identity,
type identity, token identity, identity over time, constitution, and a
biconditional criterion. Supply a non-IER example for every row.

### Module 3 — Substance, property, state, event, process, and regime

Questions

- What kinds of things can instantiate properties, undergo change, and persist?
- Can a process be a concrete particular or a subject?
- Is a regime an entity, an organization, a property of a system, or the system
  operating in a certain way?

Read

- Gateway: [Process Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/process-philosophy/),
  [Properties](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties/), and
  [Events](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/events/), selected sections.
- Core: John Dupré and Daniel J. Nicholson, “A Manifesto for a Processual
  Philosophy of Biology,” [publisher chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/27525/chapter/197490752).
- Stretch: Anne Sophie Meincke, “Persons as Biological Processes,”
  [publisher chapter](https://academic.oup.com/book/27525/chapter/197499527).

Output

Build an ontological-category test using a flame, a river current, an organism,
a software process, and a conversation. Say what persists, what changes, and
what would count as cessation in each case.

### Module 4 — Dependence relations and levels of explanation

Questions

- How do identity, supervenience, realization, constitution, grounding,
  emergence, causation, and correlation differ?
- Can one concrete event have properties described at multiple levels?
- What explanatory work is done by calling a higher-level organization real?

Read

- Gateway: [Physicalism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/physicalism/),
  sections 2.1 — 2.5, and [Metaphysical Grounding](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/grounding/).
- Core: Jaegwon Kim, “Multiple Realization and the Metaphysics of Reduction,”
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2107741).
- Stretch: Jessica Wilson, “No Work for a Theory of Grounding,”
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/0020174X.2014.907542).

Output

Describe the same non-mental case using all eight relations. For each version,
state what changes metaphysically and what evidence could distinguish it from
the alternatives.

### Module 5 — Individuation, parthood, boundaries, and levels

Questions

- What makes a collection one object or process rather than an arbitrary sum?
- Can maximality select a unique whole without first fixing the comparison
  class and scale?
- Are system boundaries discovered, caused, modeled, or stipulated?

Read

- Gateway: [Mereology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mereology/) and
  [Material Constitution](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/material-constitution/).
- Core: Peter van Inwagen, “When Are Objects Parts?”
  [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2214142).
- Stretch: Herbert Simon, “The Architecture of Complexity,”
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2786547).

Output

Give competing boundaries for a cell, an ant colony, a coupled human — tool
system, and a corporation. Identify which boundaries are causal, functional,
biological, conventional, or observer-relative.

Phase A gate — Metaphysical toolbox

Write 2,500 words answering: “What kinds of claim could a process identity
theory make?” Use only public terminology. Include one example in which
identity is plausible and one in which constitution or realization is better.

## Phase C — Subjects, unity, ownership, and time

### Module 14 — The subject and the bearer ladder

The word *bearer* must be decomposed before it is used in an argument.

| Level | Claim | What it does not yet establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B0 — Attribution | An experience is an experience of or for some subject. | Introspective self-representation. |
| B1 — Phenomenal subjectivity | Experience has for-me-ness or first-personal givenness. | A substantial self, agency, or explicit mineness. |
| B2 — Synchronic unity | Several contents belong to one total experience or subject at a time. | That subject unity explains phenomenal unity rather than the reverse. |
| B3 — Ontological bearer | Something instantiates, undergoes, or is the subject of the experience. | That this something is distinct from organized experience. |
| B4 — Diachronic bearer | One numerically identical subject persists through changing experiences. | A simple, indivisible, or immaterial substance. |
| B5 — Substantial/simple bearer | Unity is explained by a metaphysically simple subject or substance. | That organism, process, constitution, bundle, or no-self views fail. |

Read

- Core: Matthew Owen, “The Neglected Conscious Subject in Consciousness
  Science,” [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.06.009).
- Core: Raphaël Millière, “The Varieties of Selflessness,”
  [DOI](https://doi.org/10.33735/phimisci.2020.I.48), introduction and section 4.4.
- Stretch: E. J. Lowe, *Subjects of Experience*, introduction and chapters 1 — 2.

Output

Write the strongest argument for every rung and one countermodel showing why
the next rung does not follow automatically.

## The likely IER answer space — hypotheses, not conclusions

The present papers make two formulations especially important.

### Option A — System-under-a-regime identity

> A concrete physical system operating continuously as a UEF is one
> experiential subject; its UEF operation is its experience. The subject is not
> an additional bearer behind the operation. “Bearer” names the role of this
> dynamically individuated physical system in undergoing its own globally
> binding constraint.

This preserves physical monism and avoids an inner witness. It must explain why
a processually individuated system can be a subject and why subject unity is not
assumed in defining the UEF.

### Option B — Constitution without addition

> A physical system sustains a UEF; the UEF operation is identical with
> experience; and the continuously unified operation constitutes an
> experiential subject without adding a further substance.

This types the different roles more clearly. It must explain the constitution
relation and why the resulting view remains an identity theory of experience
rather than a constitution or emergence theory of the subject.

A distinct simple bearer behind the UEF would conflict with several current
denials and should be studied as a rival rather than imported accidentally
through the word *bearer*.

## Initial hypotheses to test rather than assume

1. Paper 00 identifies a real gap between adopting physicalism and identifying
   the physical event, but the novelty and scope of that problem must be tested
   against grain, mechanism, realization, and NCC literatures.
2. IER needs a bearer at least in the B0/B3 sense, but this does not entail a
   separate simple substance at B5.
3. IER’s best present route is probably to identify the subject with the
   dynamically individuated physical system operating as a UEF while
   identifying experience with that operation.
4. *System*, *regime*, *field*, *operation*, *experience*, *subject*, and
   *bearer* cannot remain freely interchangeable even if several ultimately
   co-refer.
5. The individuation and bearer problems are connected: IER cannot state what
   bears experience until it can non-circularly identify the concrete process
   doing the bearing.
6. Non-factorizability, recurrence, self-maintenance, persistence, and
   structural privacy may all be real and relevant without any one of them
   being sufficient for experience.
7. Paper 03’s strongest claim is not that created minds matter; that is already
   established territory. Its proposed contribution is that deliberate
   selection of phenomenology-structuring organization is a distinct upstream
   object of moral evaluation.
8. Paper 04 can establish a shared problem-space more easily than it can
   establish a proto-school. Social and textual evidence must supplement
   conceptual similarity.
9. Generative AI can expand the argument and counterexample space, but it does
   not supply source authority, premise truth, novelty, or responsibility.
10. IER’s hardest burden remains positive: not merely showing that its structure
    is coherent or resembles experience, but giving reason to accept identity
    over correlation, realization, constitution, emergence, criterion, or
    analogy.

Every hypothesis should be revisable by argument. None should be protected by
its usefulness to the project.
