# One Identity, Several Problems
## IER and the classic puzzles of consciousness

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.10.7)**  
*Tier-2 · Explanatory · Non-Normative · Canon-Constrained*

## Status, Scope, and Authority

This document is an explanatory map of how IER bears on several classic problems, arguments, and thought experiments concerning consciousness. It introduces no new ontological primitives, experiential criteria, thresholds, diagnostics, or grounds of ethical standing. It neither establishes nor revises the IER identity claim. It applies that claim conditionally and distinguishes what follows from it from what remains an independent philosophical or empirical question.

This document remains subordinate to the IER Specification and does not alter it.

## Orientation

The classic problems of consciousness do not constitute a single argument. They place pressure on physical theories along several different axes. Some concern the difference between describing an experience and undergoing it. Some imagine experience varying independently of physical organization. Some demand an explanation of why experience exists at all. Others ask about unity, understanding, realization, or composition.

IER therefore does not offer one interchangeable answer to every case. It offers one ontological starting point:

> Experience is identical to the operation of a physical system as a Unified Experiential Field under globally integrated intrinsic constraint.

Different consequences follow from that identity in different cases. An epistemic asymmetry may be explained without becoming an ontological difference. A proposed separation may be ruled out. A production question may be rejected as misframed. A problem generated by another ontology may never arise. A neighboring debate may be clarified without being resolved.

These differences matter. A representation is not an instantiation. Behavioral, computational, and representational equivalence are not automatically identity of intrinsic organization. Avoiding a problem is not solving it on its own premises. Making unity constitutive does not supply the empirical mechanisms by which a physical system achieves it.

## Description and Instantiation

### Mary’s Room

Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument imagines Mary, who possesses complete physical knowledge of color vision while living in a black-and-white environment. When she first sees red, she appears to learn something. The case asks whether complete physical knowledge leaves out a fact about experience.

IER accepts the epistemic change but rejects the inference from epistemic change to nonphysical ontology. Before leaving the room, Mary possesses descriptions and representations of color experience. Those representations can preserve selected organizational invariants without reproducing the represented regime. Seeing red is not the acquisition of an extra property layered onto the physical facts. It is a physical change in Mary through which the relevant experiential organization is instantiated.

IER need not settle whether Mary gains a new fact, an ability, acquaintance, or a new mode of access in order to block the anti-physicalist inference. None of those descriptions entails that what she newly knows is nonphysical. The important distinction is between representing an organization and operating as that organization.

Mary’s Room is therefore not dissolved merely by repeating the identity claim. IER explains how complete description can remain phenomenologically non-substitutive even if experience is wholly physical.

### Nagel’s Bat

Thomas Nagel argues that complete objective knowledge of a bat may still fail to reveal what it is like to be that bat. The case concerns the relation between objective description and a point of view.

IER accepts this non-substitutability. A third-person account of a bat can describe the organization of its activity without placing the knower within that organization. No description of a constraint regime is itself the system’s operation under that constraint. “What it is like” names the intrinsic bearing of the regime, not an additional object omitted from an otherwise complete inventory.

The limitation is therefore real but epistemic. Objective description and first-person instantiation are different physical and informational relations to the same kind of phenomenon. Failure of one relation to reproduce the other does not establish a second ontological domain.

### What these cases establish

Mary and the bat expose an access asymmetry. IER neither denies that asymmetry nor treats it as evidence that physical reality is incomplete. The felt gap between description and occupation is real; it does not by itself establish a gap between two kinds of entity.

## Apparent Separability

### Philosophical Zombies

A philosophical zombie is stipulated to be a complete physical duplicate of a conscious person while lacking experience. If such a duplicate is genuinely possible, physical facts do not necessitate phenomenal facts.

IER rejects the proposed combination of properties. If the duplicate preserves the complete physical organization constitutive of UEF operation, then it preserves experience by identity. If it lacks that organization, it is not a complete physical duplicate in the relevant sense.

This does not mean that behavior, intelligence, computation, or coarse functional organization guarantees experience. IER allows that systems may match a person along any of those descriptions without sustaining a UEF. The zombie argument requires more: complete physical duplication together with experiential absence. That is precisely the separation the identity claim rules out.

The result is conditional. IER shows that zombies are incompatible with its identity claim; the zombie case does not independently adjudicate whether the claim is true.

### Kripke’s Modal Argument

Saul Kripke’s modal argument places direct pressure on psychophysical identity theories. If pain is identical to a physical state, the identity appears necessary. Yet it seems conceivable that the physical state could occur without pain, or that pain could occur without that physical state. Unlike the familiar identity between water and H2O, the appearance of contingency seems difficult to explain away because pain is known through its felt character.

IER does not identify pain, or experience generally, with one named neural configuration. It identifies experience with operation as a UEF and identifies qualitative differences with differences in the organization of intrinsic constraint within that field. A case in which the full constitutive organization occurs without experience is therefore not an alternative realization of that organization under IER. It is a denial of the proposed identity.

The modal appearance remains intelligible because the concepts used on the two sides are not cognitively substitutable. One concept is applied through first-person instantiation; the other is applied through structural description. Conceivability under those distinct modes of access does not automatically establish metaphysical possibility.

This response blocks a direct move from apparent separability to ontological separability. It does not make the appearance of separability disappear, nor does it convert the identity claim into a proof of itself.

### The Inverted Spectrum

The inverted-spectrum case imagines two subjects who are behaviorally or functionally alike while the qualitative organization of their color experience is systematically reversed. The case asks whether phenomenal character can vary while the relevant physical facts remain fixed.

IER distinguishes coarse equivalence from identity of intrinsic organization. Behavioral reports, discriminations, and computational roles may remain stable while finer physical organization differs. Such a case presents no contradiction for IER: different intrinsic constraint organization can be different experience despite functional resemblance.

If the scenario instead fixes every physical relation constitutive of the UEF, inversion is excluded. Under IER, qualitative differences are differences in intrinsic constraint organization. Holding that organization completely fixed while changing qualitative character asserts both identity and non-identity of the same regime.

IER thus permits functionally hidden qualitative differences only where the underlying constitutive organization differs. It rules out an inversion that floats free of all such difference.

### Fading and Dancing Qualia

David Chalmers’s fading- and dancing-qualia arguments imagine gradual neural replacement by functionally equivalent components. Experience either fades without the subject’s functional organization registering the loss, or switches radically while functional organization remains fixed. Chalmers uses the apparent implausibility of these outcomes to support organizational invariance.

These cases do not make the same anti-physicalist move as zombies. Their force depends on what replacement preserves. IER does not treat behavioral or computational equivalence as sufficient preservation of experiential organization. A replacement could preserve external performance while changing the system’s intrinsic constraint regime. In that case an experiential transition would not be an inexplicable alteration in a separate phenomenal layer; it would be the experiential aspect of the physical reorganization itself.

If replacement preserves the complete organization constitutive of the UEF, experiential presence cannot fade independently and qualitative character cannot dance independently. If it does not preserve that organization, functional continuity alone does not determine the experiential result.

IER therefore agrees with organizational invariance only at the level of the organization it identifies with experience. It does not infer experiential invariance from implementation-independent computation or input-output function.

## Production and Explanation

### The Explanatory Gap

Joseph Levine’s explanatory gap is the persistent failure of physical and functional descriptions to make it intelligible why a state has a particular qualitative character, or why it feels like anything at all. The gap can remain even for someone who accepts physicalism.

IER separates an ontological demand from an epistemic one. The ontological demand asks for the mechanism by which an otherwise nonexperiential physical organization produces an additional experiential property. IER rejects that production relation. UEF operation does not first occur and then cause experience; that operation is experience.

The epistemic gap need not vanish. A structural description does not instantiate the regime it describes, and a third-person concept need not become cognitively interchangeable with first-person acquaintance. IER therefore blocks an ontological inference from explanatory non-substitutability without claiming that every description will convey what the corresponding experience is like.

Questions about why one intrinsic organization differs qualitatively from another, how those differences can be mapped, and how the identity can be empirically supported remain legitimate. What IER rejects is the demand for a further producer that converts the completed physical regime into experience.

### The Hard Problem

David Chalmers’s hard problem asks why and how physical or information-processing activity gives rise to subjective experience at all. It contrasts this question with functional problems concerning discrimination, integration, attention, report, and behavioral control.

IER denies the presupposed sequence. Experience is not the output of a nonexperiential process. The operation of a physical system under the organization specified by the UEF identity conditions is the experiential event. There is no additional step at which subjectivity must be attached to an otherwise complete system.

This reframes the hard problem from a production question into an identity question. It ends a regress that would otherwise demand a bridge from physical operation to an ontologically additional result. It does not end the scientific task of determining which physical systems instantiate the relevant organization, how such organization develops and changes, or how variations in its structure correspond to variations in experience.

The hard problem is therefore dissolved only in its production form. The empirical and structural study of experience remains.

## Unity and Causal Role

### The Binding Problem

“The binding problem” names several different questions. Perceptual feature binding asks how color, shape, motion, and location are correctly associated. Neural and computational binding ask how distributed processing coordinates information. Phenomenal unity asks why experience is present as one differentiated field rather than as an aggregate of independent experiences.

IER directly addresses only the last question at the ontological level. A UEF is a globally integrated regime of intrinsic constraint. Its differentiated processes participate in one experiential field; they are not independently conscious fragments that must later be combined. Unity is constitutive of the experiential regime rather than a product added after separately experiential features have formed.

This does not solve perceptual feature binding or identify the biological mechanism by which global integration is achieved. Those remain empirical questions. Nor does defining UEF operation as unified explain every local coordination process within it. IER distinguishes phenomenal unity from the mechanisms that enable and organize it.

### Mental Causation and Epiphenomenalism

Physicalist accounts are often challenged to explain how experience can be causally efficacious. If every physical event already has a physical cause, experience can appear either causally redundant or epiphenomenal. If experience is instead added as a second cause, causal competition and overdetermination arise.

IER rejects the duplication. Experiential operation and physical UEF operation are not two events competing to produce one outcome. They are one event described under different explanatory orientations. When an experiential process participates causally in action, the physical process does not perform the work on its behalf; the experiential process is that organized physical process.

This prevents epiphenomenalism generated solely by treating experience as an additional property outside the causal organization. It does not imply that every phenomenal description is an independent causal explanation, or that experience adds causal power beyond the physical operation with which it is identical.

## Neighboring Questions

The following cases matter to IER but are not all problems of phenomenal consciousness, and they are not all resolved by the experiential identity claim.

### The Chinese Room and Organized Intelligence

John Searle’s Chinese Room imagines a person manipulating Chinese symbols according to rules while understanding no Chinese. The room produces appropriate answers despite the operator’s lack of understanding. The argument primarily concerns computation, intentionality, and understanding rather than phenomenal consciousness.

IER separates organized intelligence from experiential organization. Intelligence is a functional capacity expressed in flexible problem-solving, adaptation, transfer, and context-sensitive performance. Representation preserves selected organizational invariants across transformations. Both can occur without experience, and neither is sufficient for UEF status.

The room as a whole may therefore exhibit organized intelligence and representation even though its operator does not understand Chinese. The operator’s ignorance does not establish the absence of operational understanding from the larger arrangement. At the same time, successful symbol-mediated performance does not by itself establish experiential understanding.

IER preserves a limited Searlean distinction: competence described computationally or behaviorally does not entail experience. It does not endorse the stronger inference that the whole system must lack all forms of understanding, and it does not infer experiential absence merely because a system is described as a simulation.

### Absent Qualia and Blockhead

Absent-qualia cases imagine a functionally competent system with no experience. Ned Block’s Blockhead sharpens the pressure by imagining an enormous lookup structure capable of producing every appropriate conversational response without genuine mentality.

IER allows the conceptual distinction these cases seek to expose. Organized intelligence, representational competence, and behavioral success are neither necessary nor sufficient for experience. A system may duplicate a range of functions without duplicating the intrinsic organization constitutive of a UEF.

Such a system is not automatically a philosophical zombie. A zombie is a complete physical duplicate; an absent-qualia or Blockhead case ordinarily preserves only selected functions or dispositions. IER therefore treats these cases as warnings against functional over-attribution, not as counterexamples to physical identity.

### The Combination Problem

The combination problem asks how many microexperiences or microsubjects could compose one unified macroexperience. It arises most sharply for constitutive forms of panpsychism.

IER does not posit microexperiential subjects from which a larger subject must be assembled. Experiential subjecthood obtains at the level of a unified regime. Components participate in that regime without first existing as independent subjects whose experiences must be summed.

IER therefore avoids rather than solves the panpsychist combination problem. It rejects the compositional starting point instead of showing how combination succeeds under panpsychism’s premises. This is distinct from perceptual binding, which concerns differentiation and coordination within an already unified field.

### Multiple Realizability

Multiple realizability is the thesis that the same mental or functional kind may be realized in different physical substrates. It is a constraint on theories of mind rather than a discrete puzzle of consciousness.

IER is compatible with substrate diversity but not with unrestricted implementation independence. Different materials may sustain the organization constitutive of a UEF. Shared computation, behavior, or input-output function alone does not establish that they do so. The relevant question is whether the different implementations preserve the intrinsic organization with which experience is identified.

Multiple realizability therefore does not dissolve under IER. It clarifies the level at which a claim of common realization must be assessed.

## What Follows and What Remains Open

The cases above yield several distinct results.

| Result under IER | Cases |
|---|---|
| Explains an access asymmetry without inferring ontological dualism | Mary’s Room; Nagel’s Bat |
| Rules out experiential variation if complete constitutive physical organization is fixed | Zombies; Kripkean separability; inverted spectrum; fading and dancing qualia |
| Reframes a production demand while preserving legitimate explanatory work | Explanatory gap; hard problem |
| Makes phenomenal unity constitutive while leaving enabling mechanisms empirical | Binding problem |
| Removes causal duplication by identity | Mental causation and epiphenomenalism |
| Clarifies a boundary among intelligence, representation, understanding, and experience | Chinese Room; absent qualia; Blockhead |
| Avoids a problem generated by a rival compositional ontology | Combination problem |
| Constrains implementation claims rather than posing a consciousness puzzle | Multiple realizability |

IER does not infer experience from intelligence, representation, report, semantics, computation, or behavioral similarity. It does not infer nonphysical ontology from the inability of description to reproduce instantiation. It does not permit experiential presence or character to vary independently once the complete physical organization constitutive of a UEF has been fixed. It does not posit a further mechanism that produces experience from that organization.

Several questions remain outside what this document establishes, including the empirical mechanisms that sustain global integration and the detailed mapping between qualitative variation and intrinsic constraint organization. Neither is answered merely by applying the identity claim to a classic thought experiment.

## Final Compression

The classic problems of consciousness do not share one hidden premise, and IER does not answer them all in one way. They distinguish description from instantiation, test whether experience can vary independently of physical organization, demand a production explanation, question causal efficacy and unity, and probe the relations among intelligence, representation, function, and experience.

IER supplies one ontological starting point from which several consequences follow. Epistemic asymmetries need not become ontological gaps. Complete physical duplicates cannot differ experientially. Production questions are replaced by identity. Causal competition disappears. Phenomenal unity belongs to the experiential regime itself. Problems generated by other ontologies may be avoided, while neighboring debates remain distinct.

One identity does not make every question the same. It makes possible a precise account of which questions are answered, which are reframed, which are avoided, and which remain open.
