# Evaluation Rubric

## Evaluating Theories of Experience Without Smuggling Assumptions

**Informational Experiential Realism (IER v10.10.7)**\
*Tier-3 - Evaluation Misuse Containment - Non-Normative - Canon-Constrained*

## Status, Scope, and Authority






This document establishes constraints on the fair reconstruction, evaluation,
and comparison of theories of experience. It is compatible with identity
theories, including Informational Experiential Realism (IER), but does not
privilege any theory by permitting it exemptions denied to its rivals.

It:

* introduces no new ontological commitments
* introduces no criteria for experience
* introduces no diagnostics, metrics, or tests
* introduces no evaluative verdicts
* does not argue for IER over competing theories
* does not convert IER's commitments into neutral requirements

Its purpose is methodological: to distinguish what a theory claims, what it
explains, what it leaves open, how it is investigated, and what may fairly be
inferred from it.

This document is not a scorecard. Comparative assessments belong in separate
applications.


## Why a Revised Rubric Is Needed

Theories of experience are frequently evaluated through demands that already
presuppose a rival account. Identity theories may be required to provide
observer-accessible diagnostics even when they deny that such diagnostics are
possible in principle. Access theories may be criticized for failing to answer
questions they deliberately bracket. Broad research programs may be treated as
if they made the strongest claim made by any one advocate.

The opposite error also occurs: a theory's compatibility with a position is
reported as a commitment of the theory itself. This commonly affects claims
about:

* subjecthood
* persistence and experiential gaps
* artificial consciousness
* moral standing
* reportability
* empirical certification

A fair rubric must therefore block assumption-smuggling in both directions.
It must protect every theory from imported demands while still allowing genuine
criticism of ambiguity, incompleteness, inconsistency, and unsupported
inference.


## Questions That Must Remain Distinct

At minimum, comparative work must separate the following questions:

| Level | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Explanandum | What phenomenon is the theory trying to explain? |
| Constitutive | What, according to the theory, makes an episode experiential? |
| Ontological | What does the theory claim experience is, or how is it grounded? |
| Explanatory | What dependence, identity, mechanism, or dissolution does it offer? |
| Empirical | What observable evidence bears on the theory? |
| Attributive | How, if at all, may experience be ascribed to a particular system? |
| Epistemic | What can experience or its occurrence warrant? |
| Ethical | What normative conclusions follow, if any? |

These questions may be related within a theory, but they are not interchangeable.
In particular:

* a constitutive condition is not automatically a practical diagnostic
* an empirical proxy is not automatically identical to what it measures
* behavioral report is not automatically constitutive of experience
* a neural correlate is not automatically a cause, realization, or identity
* an attribution rule is not automatically a criterion of moral standing
* compatibility with artificial realization is not a certification procedure
* an ontology does not yield ethics without a stated normative bridge


## Ontology and Epistemology

The central distinction remains:

| Level | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Ontological | What is experience, and what must reality be like for it to exist? |
| Epistemic | How could experience be known, inferred, detected, or measured? |

An identity claim may be ontologically strong while remaining epistemically
opaque. A theory may also be empirically productive without supplying a complete
ontology of experience.

Neither fact is, by itself, a defect. The evaluator must identify what the
theory claims to accomplish before judging what it does not accomplish.


## Explanation Neutrality Clause

“Explanation” in this rubric includes:

* identity
* constitution
* realization
* grounding
* causal or mechanistic production
* constraint-based necessity
* functional or representational role
* emergence
* primitiveness
* elimination or dissolution

No one explanatory form is assumed in advance. A theory may reject causal
explanation of experience, provided it states what explanatory work its
alternative performs.

Identity is a legitimate form of explanation. It is not, however, exempt from
requirements of clarity, consistency, scope, and defended necessity.


## Minimal Phenomenological Pressures

If experience exists, theories are commonly pressured to address:

* unity or multiplicity
* qualitative character or difference
* temporality
* subject-relative presence

These are comparative questions, not stipulated criteria of experience. A
theory may explain, redescribe, reject, or bracket one of them, but the evaluator
must report which response it gives.

The rubric does not specify how any of these features must be realized,
grounded, measured, or accessed.



Every substantive statement attributed to a theory must be assigned the
strongest status supported by the relevant sources.

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Entailed | Follows from the theory's explicit commitments. |
| Explicitly claimed | Asserted by the relevant author or canonical source. |
| Proposed by some advocates | Defended within the theory's literature but not constitutive of the theory. |
| Compatible | Permitted by the theory but neither required nor asserted by it. |
| Evaluator inference | A reasoned implication supplied by the evaluator and labelled as such. |
| Disputed within the theory | Varies across versions, authors, or interpretations. |
| Unspecified | Not settled by the examined sources. |
| Incompatible | Conflicts with an explicit commitment of the theory. |

These statuses must not be collapsed.

In particular:

> Compatible with does not mean entailed by.
> Suggested by an advocate does not mean part of the theory.
> Not specified does not mean denied.
> Criticized for implying does not mean self-described as claiming.

When the status is uncertain, use the weaker attribution and state the
uncertainty.


## Source and Target Discipline

### Identify the Target Precisely

Before evaluation, specify whether the target is:

* a single author's theory
* a named version of a theory
* a family of theories
* a metaphysical position
* an empirical research program
* a methodological category assembled by the evaluator

Do not write as though these targets have the same degree of unity.

If a category is heterogeneous, either select a definite member or mark the
relevant dimensions as varying by version. An evaluator-created category must
be labelled as such rather than presented as a canonical theory.

### Bound Claims by Source and Version

Each application must record:

* the source corpus used
* publication dates or archival dates where relevant
* the version of a rapidly revised theory
* whether a source is canonical, interpretive, critical, or applied
* whether terminology has changed across versions

A comparison describes the cited source state. It does not silently generalize
across every text associated with an author or program.

### Use Primary Sources for Constitutive Claims

Claims about a theory's defining commitments should be grounded, wherever
possible, in its canonical or primary sources. Secondary sources may clarify
context or disputes but should not replace primary sources when attributing a
strong commitment.

Critical interpretations must be identified as critical interpretations.

### Reconstruct Terms Locally

Shared vocabulary may conceal different meanings. Terms such as:

* identity
* information
* integration
* constraint
* representation
* access
* closure
* subject
* experience

must be reconstructed within each theory before comparison. Lexical overlap is
not theoretical convergence, and different vocabulary is not automatically
theoretical disagreement.


## Core Evaluation Dimensions

These dimensions organize inquiry. They are not requirements that every theory
must answer in the same way.

### Explanandum and Ambition

State what the theory aims to explain:

* experience as such
* conscious access
* phenomenal character
* unity or binding
* subjecthood
* cognition generally
* neural correlates or mechanisms

Distinguish the theory's stated ambition from the evaluator's preferred one.
Substituting access, reportability, intelligence, cognition, or functional
success for experience without explicit defense is a framing failure. A theory
that openly restricts its explanandum has instead made a scope choice whose
costs may be compared.

### Constitutive or Explanatory Claim

State what kind of claim the theory makes and what relation it proposes between
experience and the relevant structure, process, property, or function.

Do not use “identity,” “realization,” “grounding,” “cause,” “correlate,” and
“sufficient condition” interchangeably.

If the theory is silent or pluralist about ontology, say so.

### Account of Unity

Ask whether the theory explains experiential unity, presupposes it, treats it
as primitive, explains only information binding, or leaves it open.

If a theory claims to derive unity, identify the source of necessity. If it does
not claim such a derivation, do not impose a framework-specific necessity standard
as though it were neutral; record instead what explanatory burden remains.

### Account of Subject Individuation

Treat unity and subjecthood as separate questions. Ask whether the theory
specifies:

* what constitutes a subject
* how subjects are bounded
* whether subjects can overlap or nest
* when subjecthood begins or ends
* whether subjecthood is primitive, emergent, relational, or unspecified

Do not infer a theory of subjects merely from a theory of conscious states.
Do not infer ubiquitous subjects from ubiquitous mentality or properties.

### Qualitative Character

Ask what accounts for differences among experiences. Possible answers include
structural relations, representational content, functional organization,
primitive qualities, or explicit bracketing.

The evaluator must distinguish:

* explaining why a state is conscious
* explaining what qualitative character it has
* explaining why that character differs from another

A theory may answer one without answering all three.

### Temporal Commitments

Record what the theory explicitly says about:

* duration or temporal grain
* continuity
* persistence of a subject
* experiential gaps
* cessation and resumption

Do not manufacture a persistence theory from a state-level constitutive claim.
The absence of a required state at one time does not, without further argument,
settle subject persistence, gaps, or later numerical identity.

### Epistemic Commitments

Ask whether experience is claimed to confer truth, correctness, introspective
authority, or privileged access. Also ask whether the theory treats observer
inference as possible, limited, or impossible.

Epistemic non-authority is a live theoretical position. Epistemic opacity is not
by itself an ontological defect.

### Empirical Operationalization

Record separately:

* data used to develop or test the theory
* operational definitions used in experiments
* behavioral or neural measures
* proxies and their validation
* practical limits on measurement

Do not present an in-principle constitutive analysis as though it were a
currently feasible measurement procedure. Do not present a proxy as direct
measurement unless the theory and evidence warrant that equivalence.

### Third-Person Attribution

Ask what, if anything, licenses attribution of experience to a particular
system. Distinguish:

* theoretical constitution
* evidence of constitution
* probabilistic attribution
* behavioral evidence
* report
* metaphysical assumption
* practical certification

If the theory supplies no attribution procedure, report that absence without
automatically treating it as refutation.

### Defeat and Revision Conditions

State defeat conditions at the level appropriate to the theory. These may
include:

* conceptual inconsistency
* failure of a claimed derivation or necessity
* empirical results contrary to a prediction
* failure of a proposed proxy
* displacement by a better constitutive or identity account
* internal inability to distinguish cases the theory claims to distinguish

Experimental diagnosability is required only when claimed. A theory must not,
however, be protected from every possible revision by shifting between
ontological, empirical, and methodological claims.

### Substrate Constraints and Artificial Realization

Distinguish:

* substrate-neutral in principle
* biologically constrained
* dependent on a particular physical organization
* compatible with artificial realization
* claimed sufficient for artificial consciousness
* equipped with a practical certification procedure
* silent on artificial consciousness

The first five do not entail the sixth. “Could be conscious if” must not be
presented as “can be certified as conscious by.”

### Ethical Implications

Identify separately:

1. the theory's account of experience or subjecthood
2. any normative bridge principle
3. the resulting claim about moral standing, welfare, responsibility, or harm

Unless all three are supplied, the ethical conclusion is not entailed by the
theory alone. Ethical proposals by advocates must be labelled as applications.

Ethical comfort or discomfort is not evidence for or against an ontology.


## Comparative Application Protocol

Applications to named theories must proceed in five stages.

### Stage 1 - Define the Target

Name the theory, version, authors or family boundaries, source corpus, and
explanandum.

### Stage 2 - Reconstruct Before Evaluating

Present the theory in terms its proponents could reasonably endorse. Separate:

* explicit commitments
* internal disputes
* omissions
* evaluator inference

Use the claim-status labels in section 3.

### Stage 3 - Evaluate Internally

Ask whether the theory is clear, coherent, adequately supported on its own
terms, and successful by its own stated ambitions. Distinguish:

* contradiction
* explanatory incompleteness
* acknowledged scope limitation
* rejection of the demand

These are not the same criticism.

### Stage 4 - Compare Matched Claims

Compare like with like:

* constitution with constitution
* ontology with ontology
* empirical prediction with empirical prediction
* attribution method with attribution method
* ethical entailment with ethical entailment

Do not compare one theory's metaphysics with another theory's experimental
proxy as though they were competing answers at the same level.

Record:

* strongest genuine convergence
* identity-level divergence
* criterial or constitutive divergence
* epistemic divergence
* empirical divergence
* unresolved or incommensurable questions

“Incommensurable” should be used only where no matched claim can be identified,
not merely because the theories use different vocabularies or methods.

### Stage 5 - State Comparative Costs Without Verdict Inflation

State what each theory gains, leaves unexplained, rules out, or makes difficult.
A comparative advantage is not automatically a refutation. A missing answer is
not automatically a contradiction. A disagreement is empirically rivalrous if
the theories make incompatible claims about observable outcomes; it may remain
ontological or conceptual otherwise.


## Required Comparison Record

For each theory, a rigorous application should include:

| Field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Target | Exact theory, family, author, and version |
| Sources | Primary corpus and relevant secondary interpretation |
| Explanandum | What the theory claims to explain |
| Constitutive/explanatory claim | Exact relation asserted |
| Unity | Claimed account, primitive status, or omission |
| Subject individuation | Claimed account, variation, or omission |
| Qualitative character | What fixes conscious character, if specified |
| Temporal commitments | Only what sources support |
| Empirical operationalization | Measures, proxies, and limits |
| Third-person attribution | Evidence or procedure, if any |
| Defeat conditions | Conceptual and empirical, as applicable |
| Artificial realization | Constraint, compatibility, sufficiency, or silence |
| Ethical implications | Entailment, added bridge, application, or silence |
| Claim status | Entailed, explicit, advocated, compatible, inferred, disputed, unspecified, or incompatible |
| IER relation | Convergence and divergences separated by level |
| Revision trigger | Evidence or scholarship that would require updating the profile |

“Unspecified” is an acceptable and often necessary entry.


## Adversarial-Method Discipline

Where theories make empirical predictions, comparative work should adopt the
discipline of adversarial collaboration where applicable:

* formulate each side's commitment in terms its proponents could endorse
* identify discriminating rather than merely confirmatory consequences
* specify expected interpretations before treating evidence as decisive
* allow mixed results to challenge both theories
* keep theory-neutral adjudication distinct from theory advocacy

This discipline does not impose experimental diagnosability on theories that
reject it. For an identity theory such as IER, conceptual inconsistency, failure
of claimed necessity, or displacement by a rival identity claim remain
legitimate defeat conditions even where no third-person consciousness test is
licensed.


## Core Principles

### Principle 1 - Explanandum Discipline

State what the theory explains before evaluating whether it succeeds.

### Principle 2 - Claim-Strength Discipline

Attribute no claim more strongly than the sources support.

### Principle 3 - Target Discipline

Do not treat a family, research program, metaphysical position, or
evaluator-created category as a single theory without qualification.

### Principle 4 - Relational Discipline

Do not conflate identity, grounding, realization, causation, correlation,
constitution, sufficiency, and evidence.

### Principle 5 - Level Discipline

Keep ontological, constitutive, empirical, attributive, epistemic, and ethical
claims separate unless the theory explicitly connects them.

### Principle 6 - Symmetrical Charity

Apply the same standards of source fidelity, qualification, and burden of proof
to IER and to every rival.

### Principle 7 - Honest Omission

Report silence as silence. Do not fill a theory's gaps with convenient
implications, and do not treat every silence as a contradiction.

### Principle 8 - Appropriate Defeat Conditions

Evaluate each claim at the level where it is made while preventing movement
between levels from immunizing the theory against revision.

### Principle 9 - Ethical Bridge Discipline

Do not derive moral status or policy directly from a consciousness theory
without identifying the normative premise that connects them.

### Principle 10 - Evaluative Responsibility

Distinguish a theory failing its own commitments from a theory refusing the
evaluator's preferred question. Distinguish both from a genuine comparative
cost.


## Common Evaluation Errors This Rubric Blocks

This method is designed to prevent:

* penalizing identity theories merely for refusing diagnostics
* rewarding operational tractability as though it settled ontology
* confusing access or report with experience without argument
* treating a constitutive criterion as a practical measurement method
* converting empirical proxies into identities
* inferring subjecthood from state consciousness
* inferring persistence from momentary conditions
* treating theory families as uniform
* projecting one advocate's position onto an entire theory
* converting compatibility into commitment
* converting silence into denial
* treating artificial realizability as artificial-consciousness certification
* deriving moral status without a normative bridge
* representing IER's own requirements as neutral requirements of every theory
* declaring theories non-rivalrous before checking for incompatible matched claims


## What This Rubric Evaluates

The rubric evaluates whether a comparative account:

* identifies its target and sources precisely
* reconstructs the target accurately and charitably
* states claim strength explicitly
* separates levels of analysis
* identifies genuine explanatory commitments and omissions
* evaluates internal coherence under the theory's own commitments
* compares matched claims rather than unlike categories
* distinguishes theory from application
* states uncertainty and revision conditions honestly

It also permits substantive criticism where a theory equivocates, contradicts
itself, overstates its evidence, fails a claimed derivation, or leaves a burden
unanswered that follows from its own ambition.


## What This Rubric Does Not Decide

This rubric does not decide:

* which theory is true
* whether every theory must answer every dimension
* which explanatory costs are acceptable
* whether epistemic opacity is practically tolerable
* whether empirical tractability outweighs ontological ambition
* how policy or engineering should proceed under uncertainty

It determines whether a theory has been reconstructed and compared without
smuggling commitments across theories, levels, or domains.


## Orientation Statement

Fair evaluation is neither deference nor neutrality by vagueness. It requires
precise attribution, matched comparison, and criticism at the level where a
claim is actually made.

> Judge each theory by what it claims, distinguish what it permits from what
> it entails, and compare only answers to the same question.
